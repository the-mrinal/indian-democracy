<Person1>Hi Aditya. Today's the one I've been waiting for — President, Vice-President, Prime Minister, Cabinet. Because every Republic Day I watch the President take the salute on Rajpath and I genuinely cannot tell you what she does the other 364 days.</Person1>
<Person2>Hi Neha. That's actually the whole chapter in one sentence. The Constitution gives the President an enormous list of powers — Article 53 says the entire executive authority of the Union is vested in her. And then Article 74 quietly takes most of it back. She acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers. So the textbook President and the lived President are two different people, and the gap between them is where Indian politics actually happens.</Person2>
<Person1>Droupadi Murmu (DROH-pa-dee MOOR-moo) — that's our current President since July 2022. Fifteenth. First tribal President, second woman, and the first one born after independence, which I hadn't really registered.</Person1>
<Person2>That last bit is the quietly remarkable one. Every previous President had a memory of colonial India. Murmu doesn't. And she won that office through a system most Indians have never had to think about — because we don't vote for the President. About four thousand eight hundred people do.</Person2>
<Person1>Right, the Electoral College. Walk me through who's actually in that room. Because I always assumed it was just Parliament.</Person1>
<Person2>It's three groups. The elected MPs of both Houses — so Lok Sabha (lohk SUB-hah) and Rajya Sabha (RAHJ-yuh SUB-hah). The elected MLAs of all 28 states. And the elected MLAs of three union territories — Delhi, Puducherry, and Jammu and Kashmir. Nominated members don't vote. MLCs from the legislative councils don't vote either. Only people the people actually chose.</Person2>
<Person1>And each vote is weighted. This is the part I find genuinely beautiful in a nerdy way. My MLA in Bangalore doesn't carry the same weight as an MLA from UP.</Person1>
<Person2>Two formulas, both running off the 1971 census. An MLA's vote is worth her state's 1971 population divided by the number of elected MLAs, divided by a thousand. So a UP MLA carries about 208 votes. A Sikkim MLA carries about 7. Then every MP's vote is calibrated so that the total weight of all MPs equals the total weight of all MLAs put together. Parity between the Union and the states. In 2022, an MP's vote was worth 700.</Person2>
<Person1>The 1971 freeze is doing a lot of work in that formula. That number is older than I am. Older than you are.</Person1>
<Person2>Marginally older than me, thank you. But yes — frozen by the 42nd Amendment during the Emergency, extended again by the 84th Amendment, and the current deadline is the first census after 2026. The original reason was to not punish southern states that had succeeded at population control. Whether that bargain holds when delimitation finally happens is one of the big constitutional fights of the next decade.</Person2>
<Person1>So the President is elected by this weighted college. And then the Constitution hands her a wishlist. Appoints the PM, summons Parliament, signs every bill, supreme commander of the armed forces, can declare three kinds of emergency, can pardon a death sentence under Article 72. That is a lot.</Person1>
<Person2>It reads like a monarch's job description. And it was deliberately written that way, because the framers were borrowing the Westminster model — vest formal power in a head of state, then bind that head of state by convention to act on ministerial advice. The 42nd and 44th Amendments turned the convention into hard text. The President can return advice once for reconsideration. If the Cabinet sends it back, she has to sign.</Person2>
<Person1>Has any President actually pushed back? Or is it all theatre?</Person1>
<Person2>It's mostly theatre, but not entirely. The chapter doesn't get into specific instances, but the reserve powers it does flag — picking a PM when no party has a majority, deciding on President's Rule, sitting on a Bill — those are the moments where a President's judgment actually matters. Most of the time, though, the most consequential thing the office does is what you described. Take the salute. Read the Republic Day speech someone else wrote. Sign.</Person2>
<Person1>And if a President does go rogue, impeachment under Article 61. Which has never happened.</Person1>
<Person2>Never. And the bar is deliberately high. The only ground is "violation of the Constitution" — which is undefined, by design. A quarter of one House has to sign the motion. Fourteen days' notice. Two-thirds of the total membership of that House has to pass it. Then the other House investigates and also has to pass it by two-thirds. And here's the quirk — nominated MPs, who can't vote to elect the President, can vote to remove her.</Person2>
<Person1>Move us to the Vice-President. Because something genuinely unusual happened last year — Jagdeep Dhankhar resigned mid-term in July 2025.</Person1>
<Person2>Only the third early vacancy in our history. He cited health, stepped down on the 21st of July, and we held a by-election on the 9th of September — the first VP by-election since 1987. C.P. Radhakrishnan won, took oath as the 15th VP on the 12th of September. And the by-election rolled out almost mechanically, which is the point. The office doesn't sit vacant.</Person2>
<Person1>The VP's electoral college is different from the President's, which I had not appreciated until I read this chapter.</Person1>
<Person2>Completely different. Only Parliament votes — both Houses — and both elected and nominated members get a ballot. No state legislators at all. Which makes sense once you remember the VP's actual day job. He chairs the Rajya Sabha. So the body he presides over is the body that has the loudest voice in choosing him.</Person2>
<Person1>And his removal is the strangest procedure in the Constitution. It's the only office removable by what the chapter calls an "effective majority."</Person1>
<Person2>It's the only one. Not a special two-thirds majority, not a simple majority of those present. A majority of all the then members of the Rajya Sabha — so abstentions and absences count against you. The Lok Sabha then has to agree by simple majority. And critically, only the Rajya Sabha can initiate it. The Lok Sabha can't even start the conversation about removing its own chairman's counterpart.</Person2>
<Person1>Now the part that actually decides who runs the country. The PM.</Person1>
<Person2>And the constitutional text on this is almost embarrassingly thin. Article 75 — the PM is appointed by the President. That's it. There's no qualification, no procedure, no list of duties. The entire institution runs on the convention that the President calls the leader of the party or coalition with a majority in the Lok Sabha. Everything else — the PMO, Cabinet committees, the categories of ministers — is convention or sub-constitutional rule.</Person2>
<Person1>Narendra Modi was sworn in for his third term on the 9th of June 2024. The chapter notes he's only the second person after Nehru (NEH-roo) to do three consecutive terms.</Person1>
<Person2>That's the historical bookmark. Nehru did three consecutive terms from 1952 onward. Indira Gandhi did three terms but not consecutively — there was the 1977 gap after the Emergency. Modi joins Nehru on that specific metric. And the 2024 Cabinet that came out of it has about 72 ministers, which sits well under the cap.</Person2>
<Person1>The cap is the 91st Amendment, 2003. Total ministers including the PM can't exceed 15 percent of Lok Sabha strength. So for a 543-seat House, that's about 81. Why did we even need a cap?</Person1>
<Person2>Because before 2003, state governments in particular were inflating Cabinets to the size of a small wedding. Every defecting MLA who needed to be kept happy got a ministry. The 91st Amendment was passed during the Vajpayee government as part of the broader anti-defection cleanup — and it did two things in one go. It capped the size, and it said any MLA or MP disqualified under the Tenth Schedule for defecting can't be made a minister until they get re-elected. You can't defect your way into a Cabinet anymore.</Person2>
<Person1>And the Council of Ministers itself has three tiers, which the chapter is careful to point out is convention, not Constitution.</Person1>
<Person2>Right. Cabinet Ministers — the senior ones with portfolios who actually sit in Cabinet. Ministers of State with Independent Charge — they run a department but don't ex officio attend Cabinet. And Ministers of State — who assist the Cabinet Minister in their ministry. The Constitution sees only one Council. The three-layer cake is a creature of practice.</Person2>
<Person1>The bit that ties them all together is Article 75(3). Collective responsibility to the Lok Sabha. Every Indian has heard the phrase. What does it mean in practice?</Person1>
<Person2>Two things. Externally — if a no-confidence motion passes the Lok Sabha, the entire ministry falls. Not just the minister in charge of whatever caused the crisis. The whole council. Internally — once Cabinet takes a decision by majority, every minister has to defend it in public. If you can't, your only option is to resign. You don't get to be a Cabinet minister and publicly disagree with Cabinet. That's not a thing.</Person2>
<Person1>And the actual decision-making in modern government has shifted into Cabinet Committees. The Modi government reconstituted eight of them on the 3rd of July 2024.</Person1>
<Person2>And the PM chairs six of those eight. The two everyone has heard of are the Cabinet Committee on Security — PM, Home, Finance, Defence, External Affairs — which signs off on everything from a defence procurement to a counter-terror operation. And the Cabinet Committee on Political Affairs, which the chapter calls the "super-Cabinet." Major political calls get resolved there before they ever reach the full Cabinet. The Appointments Committee — PM and Home Minister — decides every senior bureaucratic posting in the country. That's where a lot of real power actually sits.</Person2>
<Person1>And then there's the PMO, which has become a parallel power centre in its own right. P.K. Mishra as Principal Secretary, Ajit Doval as NSA, both Cabinet rank.</Person1>
<Person2>Both continuing into Modi 3.0. Mishra's been heading the PMO since 2019, Doval has been NSA since 2014 — that's unusually long tenures for offices that are technically at the PM's pleasure. The PMO isn't a constitutional body. It's an office. But it's the office through which every important file in the Union government now flows.</Person2>
<Person1>One office we haven't touched — the Attorney General. Article 76. R. Venkataramani got reappointed in October 2025 for another two years.</Person1>
<Person2>Highest law officer of the Union. Appointed by the President, holds office at her pleasure, must be qualified to be a Supreme Court judge. The AG has the right of audience in every court in India — which no other lawyer does. He can sit in Parliament and speak, but can't vote. The Solicitor General and the Additional Solicitors General assist him, but those are statutory posts, not constitutional ones.</Person2>
<Person1>So if you zoom out — the President is the constitutional head, the PM is the political head, the Cabinet is the working body, the PMO and Cabinet Committees are where decisions actually get made, and the AG is the lawyer who defends all of it.</Person1>
<Person2>And the thread running through every one of those offices is the same: where the text says one thing, convention does something else. The President shall act. The PM shall be appointed. The ministers shall hold office during pleasure. Read literally, none of it tells you who actually runs India. You only find that out by watching the practice.</Person2>
<Person1>Which leaves the question I keep coming back to. When does that gap between the text and the practice actually matter? Because the day a President decides to test her reserve powers — a hung Lok Sabha, a controversial bill, a request to dissolve Parliament — that gap stops being academic.</Person1>
<Person2>And that's the day the textbook becomes the Constitution again. The reason no one talks about Article 53 most of the time is that no one needs to. The day they do, the whole arrangement we just described is suddenly on the table. Which is why every coalition era in Indian politics has been, quietly, a presidency era too.</Person2>
