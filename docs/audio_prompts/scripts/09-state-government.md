<Person1>Hi Aditya. Today's the layer of government that actually shows up in your life — state government. The traffic cop who waves you down at the signal, the government school your cousin teaches at, the pothole on your street that nobody fills. Police, roads, schools, hospitals, agriculture — almost everything you call "government" in daily life runs through the state. And I want to start with the office most people don't think about until there's a fight: the Governor.</Person1>
<Person2>Hi Neha. Good place to start, because in 2025 the Supreme Court did something it had never done in seventy-five years of the Constitution. The Tamil Nadu Governor, R.N. Ravi, had been sitting on ten state bills — some pending since January 2020. In April 2025, a bench of Justices Pardiwala and Mahadevan invoked Article 142 and just deemed those ten bills to have received assent. The court signed them, in effect. That's the headline that should be in this episode's opening, because it tells you the Governor's pocket-veto era is over.</Person2>
<Person1>Hold on. Before we get to the fight — explain to me why the Governor is even in this position. Why does a state, with its own elected CM and Assembly, need someone appointed by the President of India sitting in the Raj Bhavan?</Person1>
<Person2>Because the design is parliamentary, and parliamentary systems need a constitutional head who is separate from the political executive. At the Centre, that's the President. At the state, it's the Governor. Articles 153 to 162. Appointed by the President under Article 155 — eligibility is basically: Indian citizen, thirty-five years old. There was a convention that the Governor shouldn't be from the state they're posted to, to keep them impartial. Honoured in the breach, mostly.</Person2>
<Person1>And they serve at the President's pleasure, which sounds polite but actually means the Union government can recall them whenever it wants.</Person1>
<Person2>That was the assumption until 2010. B.P. Singhal v. Union of India — a Constitution Bench said no, the pleasure doctrine is not unfettered. A Governor can't be removed just because they're "out of sync" with the Centre, or because the government at the Centre changed. The court called the Governor "not an agent or employee of the Union." Which is a lovely sentence. It's also a sentence that's been tested repeatedly ever since.</Person2>
<Person1>Tested how, in practice? Because every time there's an opposition-ruled state, you hear the same complaint — Governor sitting on bills, Governor refusing to swear in ministers, Governor making speeches against the state government.</Person1>
<Person2>The complaint has a long pedigree. But the legal pushback is recent. November 2023, State of Punjab v. Principal Secretary to the Governor — Chief Justice Chandrachud's bench told the Punjab Governor he could not "doubt the validity of an Assembly session" and could not "thwart the normal course of lawmaking." The phrase the court used was "symbolic head." Then comes the Tamil Nadu judgment in April 2025, which goes further. It says under Article 200, the Governor does not have an absolute or pocket veto. Indicative timelines — one month with ministerial advice, three months without, one month for re-passed bills. And once a bill is re-passed by the Assembly after reconsideration, the Governor is constitutionally bound to assent. Bound. Not "should." Bound.</Person2>
<Person1>So the loophole that everyone exploited — just don't sign, don't say no, don't say anything, let it rot on the desk — that's closed?</Person1>
<Person2>Substantially closed. Whether it's permanently closed depends on whether future benches uphold the timelines, and whether the Centre tries to amend Article 200. But for now, yes.</Person2>
<Person1>Okay, the Governor signs — or is deemed to sign. Who's actually running the state? The CM and her Council of Ministers. Mirror of the PM-Cabinet model at the Centre.</Person1>
<Person2>Same architecture. Article 164. The Governor appoints the CM — by convention, the leader of the party or coalition with a majority in the Vidhan Sabha (VID-hahn SUB-hah). Other ministers are appointed on the CM's advice. They hold office during the Governor's pleasure, but in real life, it's the CM's pleasure that matters. And there's a cap most people don't know about — Article 164(1A), inserted by the 91st Amendment in 2003. Total ministers cannot exceed fifteen per cent of the Assembly's strength, with a minimum of twelve.</Person2>
<Person1>Why fifteen per cent? Where does that number come from?</Person1>
<Person2>It came after the defection era of the 1990s and early 2000s, where chief ministers were essentially bribing MLAs with ministerial berths to keep coalitions stitched together. You'd see cabinets of seventy, eighty people in some states. The 91st Amendment did two things — capped the size, and barred any MLA disqualified under the Tenth Schedule from being made a minister. Article 164(1B). Same amendment. It was an attempt to make defection unprofitable in two directions at once.</Person2>
<Person1>Did it work?</Person1>
<Person2>It made buying loyalty more expensive. It didn't make it impossible. Ask Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra in the last decade.</Person2>
<Person1>Let's talk about the Assembly itself, because the variation between states is wild. Sikkim has thirty-two MLAs. Uttar Pradesh has four hundred and three.</Person1>
<Person2>Article 170 sets the band — between sixty and five hundred members, elected by First Past the Post from territorial constituencies, five-year terms. The size tracks population, roughly. So UP at 403, Maharashtra 288, West Bengal 294, Bihar 243, Tamil Nadu 234, Madhya Pradesh 230 at the big end. Goa at 40, Mizoram at 40, Sikkim at 32 at the small end. SC and ST seats are reserved under Article 332, proportional to population in each state.</Person2>
<Person1>And the Anglo-Indian nomination is gone — same 104th Amendment that ended it at the Centre.</Person1>
<Person2>Effective 25 January 2020. Quietly, no protests. The community itself had shrunk to a point where the rationale was thin.</Person2>
<Person1>Now the bit I never understood — the second chamber. Only six states have one. Why those six?</Person1>
<Person2>Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra, Bihar, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana. There's no deep constitutional reason — Article 169 lets Parliament create or abolish a Vidhan Parishad (VID-hahn pa-ri-SHAD) if the state Assembly passes a resolution by special majority. So it's been a political choice, state by state. Tamil Nadu had one, abolished it, has been talking about reviving it for years. Andhra abolished theirs in the 1980s and revived it in 2007.</Person2>
<Person1>And the composition of the Council is genuinely strange. Graduates of three years' standing elect some members. Teachers elect some. It's like someone designed a chamber for a 1950s civic society.</Person1>
<Person2>That's almost exactly what happened. One-third elected by local body members — municipalities, district boards. One-twelfth by graduates. One-twelfth by teachers. One-third by the MLAs themselves. One-sixth nominated by the Governor for literature, science, art, cooperative movement, social service. It is a chamber for a 1950s civic society. The graduate constituency was meant to bring in educated voices when the franchise was new. The teacher constituency similarly. Whether that logic survives today — debatable.</Person2>
<Person1>How much power does the Council actually have? Because the Rajya Sabha can block things at the Centre.</Person1>
<Person2>The Vidhan Parishad is much weaker than the Rajya Sabha. On an ordinary bill, if the Council rejects or amends what the Assembly sent, the Assembly waits a month, re-passes it, and after a second passage the bill is deemed passed. So the Council is, at most, a four-month delaying chamber. On Money Bills, it can only recommend — the Assembly is free to ignore. And there's no joint sitting mechanism at the state level. The Assembly always wins eventually.</Person2>
<Person1>Which makes you wonder why the chamber exists at all in some of these states.</Person1>
<Person2>It exists partly as a soft landing for senior politicians who lost an Assembly seat, partly to bring in expertise without contesting an election, and partly as patronage. Useful, sometimes. Necessary, rarely.</Person2>
<Person1>Let me bring in something concrete. Article 371. This is the constitutional fine print that most people don't read, but it's why states like Nagaland and Mizoram have rules around land and customary law that don't apply elsewhere.</Person1>
<Person2>371-A for Nagaland — customary law and procedure, land transfers, oil and gas resources protected. 371-G for Mizoram, similar. 371-F for Sikkim after the 1975 merger. 371-H gives the Arunachal Pradesh Governor a special responsibility for law and order. 371-J for the Hyderabad-Karnataka region. These aren't asymmetries that the Constitution stumbled into. They were negotiated, one by one, often after long political battles or insurgencies. The Constitution accommodates difference — it doesn't pretend India is uniform.</Person2>
<Person1>And then there's Article 370. Which was read down on 5 August 2019, and the Supreme Court upheld the abrogation in December 2023.</Person1>
<Person2>In Re: Article 370. The most consequential constitutional moment of recent years, and one of the few where the court signed off on a major executive action by the Centre against a state's special status. The other special provisions — 371-A through J — were not touched. So far.</Person2>
<Person1>Last thing. President's Rule. Article 356. The nuclear option.</Person1>
<Person2>The President assumes the state government's functions, the Council of Ministers stands dismissed, the Assembly is suspended or dissolved. Parliament has to approve within two months. Six months at a time, three years maximum under special conditions. It was abused routinely from the 1950s through the 1980s — Indira Gandhi imposed it more than any other Prime Minister, dozens of times, often to dislodge opposition-ruled state governments. S.R. Bommai v. Union of India in 1994 changed that. The court brought the proclamation under judicial review, and said the majority of the Council of Ministers has to be tested on the floor of the Assembly. Not in the Raj Bhavan, not in some letter from the Governor to the President. On the floor, with members voting.</Person2>
<Person1>And usage has collapsed since Bommai.</Person1>
<Person2>From a routine political tool to a rarely-used emergency lever. The states have learned, and the Centre has learned, that the floor test is the only real test. You can't dismiss a government over breakfast in Raj Bhavan and have the Supreme Court not ask questions by lunch.</Person2>
<Person1>So if I zoom out — between Bommai in 1994 and the Tamil Nadu judgment in 2025, there's a pattern. The court has been slowly draining the discretionary water out of the Governor's office. Pleasure isn't unfettered. Dismissal needs a floor test. Withholding assent has a clock on it. The Governor is becoming what the chapter calls a symbolic head, in practice, not just on paper.</Person1>
<Person2>And every one of those limits the court has imposed has been on executive convention, not on a clear constitutional text. Article 200 still says "Governor may withhold assent." The court read a clock into it. A clock that wasn't written.</Person2>
<Person1>Which is the next constitutional fight, somewhere down the road. Whether a deemed assent under Article 142 is something the Centre will accept the next time it happens. Or whether the doctrine ends in a referral to a larger bench and quietly disappears, the way so many uncomfortable doctrines in this country have a habit of doing.</Person1>
