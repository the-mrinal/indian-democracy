<Person1>Hi Aditya. I want to start somewhere unexpected today — with one word in Article 1 of the Constitution. The word is "Union." Not "Federation." That choice is doing a lot of work.</Person1>
<Person2>Hi Neha. It's a great place to start, because Ambedkar (AHM-bed-kar) actually defended that wording on the floor of the Constituent Assembly. He said the Drafting Committee deliberately avoided "Federation of States" for three reasons. The Indian Union is not the result of an agreement between states. States have no right to secede. And the Union is indestructible, even if individual states are not. That's the entire federal philosophy compressed into one noun.</Person2>
<Person1>So unlike the United States, where thirteen colonies signed up to a deal, the Indian states didn't choose to join. They were already part of something the Constitution then defined.</Person1>
<Person2>Right. And that's why K.C. Wheare called India "quasi-federal" — federal in form, unitary in spirit. The Supreme Court did eventually push back on that in S.R. Bommai in 1994, holding that federalism is part of the basic structure of the Constitution. But the design itself? The design tilts hard toward the Centre.</Person2>
<Person1>Let's count the territorial units, because this changes more than people realise. As of today, June 2026, it's 28 states and 8 Union Territories.</Person1>
<Person2>And both those numbers moved within living memory. Telangana was carved out of Andhra in 2014. J&K stopped being a state in October 2019 — split into two UTs, Jammu and Kashmir with an assembly, and Ladakh (luh-DAAK) without one. Then in January 2020, the two small UTs on the west coast — Dadra and Nagar Haveli, and Daman and Diu — were merged into one. The map is not a fixed thing in India. Parliament can redraw it.</Person2>
<Person1>That's actually the part that surprises me most. Article 3 — Parliament can alter a state's boundaries without that state's ratification. The state legislature gets consulted, but its opinion isn't binding.</Person1>
<Person2>Try imagining the US Congress redrawing California's borders over Sacramento's objections. It's unthinkable there. Here, it's a routine constitutional power. And of those 8 UTs, only three actually have elected assemblies — Delhi since 1991, Puducherry (pun-duh-CHER-ee), and now J&K, which finally got its first post-reorganisation election in September–October 2024 after five years of direct central rule.</Person2>
<Person1>Okay, so where does the actual division of power live? Because someone listening to this is filling out a GST form on one app and renewing a driving licence on another, and they're not always sure which government is in charge of what.</Person1>
<Person2>That's the Seventh Schedule. Three lists. The Union List has about 98 subjects — defence, foreign affairs, atomic energy, railways, banking, currency, citizenship. The big-stakes stuff. The State List has about 59 — police, public health, agriculture, land, liquor, local government. And the Concurrent List has 47 — criminal law, marriage, education, electricity, forests, where both can legislate.</Person2>
<Person1>And if they conflict on a concurrent subject?</Person1>
<Person2>Union law prevails. Article 254. There's a narrow safety valve in 254(2) where a state law passed with the President's assent can prevail inside that state — but that assent is given by the Centre, so the Centre still holds the key. And residuary powers — anything nobody thought to list — go to Parliament under Article 248. The unlisted defaults belong to Delhi, not to Lucknow or Chennai.</Person2>
<Person1>I'd push back a little here, though. On paper that sounds overwhelming for the Centre. But in practice the State List has the things that touch ordinary life every single day. Your kid's school, the cop at the chowki (CHOW-kee), the dispensary, who owns the land your house sits on.</Person1>
<Person2>Fair qualification. The Union List has the headline subjects, the State List has the everyday ones. That's actually a good description of why Indian federalism is more lived-in than it looks on paper. But then there are five windows where Parliament can climb into the State List anyway — Article 249 if Rajya Sabha votes by two-thirds in national interest, Article 250 during a national emergency, Article 252 if states themselves request it, Article 253 to implement an international treaty, and Article 356 during President's Rule.</Person2>
<Person1>That last one — 356 — has the most political baggage. President's Rule. Dismissing an elected state government. Indira Gandhi (IN-dih-rah GAHN-dee) and her successors used it dozens of times in the seventies and eighties.</Person1>
<Person2>It was practically a remote control for the Centre. And then Bommai in 1994 changed the rules. The Supreme Court said three things that mattered. The proclamation is subject to judicial review. The "majority" test has to happen on the floor of the Assembly, not in the Raj Bhavan (RAJ buh-VAN) with the Governor counting heads in private. And if the court strikes down the proclamation, the dissolved Assembly can actually be revived.</Person2>
<Person1>Reviving a dissolved Assembly. That's an extraordinary remedy.</Person1>
<Person2>It is. And since Bommai, the use of 356 has fallen off a cliff. From a routine political tool to a rarely-used emergency lever. That's one of the clearest cases of a court verdict actually changing how the political class behaves. The other design features the chapter lists are just as load-bearing — single Constitution, single citizenship, an integrated judiciary with the Supreme Court at the apex rather than the US-style dual system, and the All-India Services. The IAS officer in your district collector's office was recruited by the Union Public Service Commission, then allotted to a state cadre. So even the bureaucracy running your state isn't really hired by your state.</Person2>
<Person1>Which brings us to the Governor. This is the role that keeps showing up in headlines and I think most listeners aren't quite sure what a Governor even does day-to-day.</Person1>
<Person2>Constitutionally, the Governor is the head of the state. Politically, the Governor is appointed by the President — which means, in practice, by the Union Council of Ministers — and removable at the President's pleasure. So when the party at the Centre is different from the party in the state, the Governor can start to look less like a constitutional umpire and more like a Union appointee parked in enemy territory.</Person2>
<Person1>And the friction shows up in one specific way — Governors sitting on bills passed by the state assembly. Just not signing them. Forever.</Person1>
<Person2>The pocket veto. Which, until very recently, the Constitution didn't really have a clear answer for. Article 200 says the Governor "shall" give assent, withhold it, or reserve it for the President. It doesn't say when. So some Governors discovered an unofficial fourth option — do nothing, indefinitely.</Person2>
<Person1>Tamil Nadu took that fight all the way up.</Person1>
<Person2>And won, in April 2025. Justices Pardiwala and Mahadevan held that Governor R.N. Ravi's withholding of ten state bills — some pending since January 2020 — was illegal and erroneous. The Court fixed actual timelines for the first time. One month if the Governor is acting on ministerial advice. Three months without. One month for re-passed bills. And then, using Article 142, it did something unprecedented — it deemed those ten bills to have received the Governor's assent. The Court itself signed them, in effect.</Person2>
<Person1>That's a remarkable remedy. The court basically said: if you won't do your job, we'll do it for you.</Person1>
<Person2>It closes the pocket veto loophole at the state level for the first time. Combined with the 2010 Singhal judgment — which said a Governor can't be removed just because a new party came to power at the Centre — the Governor's job has finally been pulled, kicking and screaming, from a discretionary political post into a constitutionally constrained role.</Person2>
<Person1>Speaking of constrained roles — Delhi. This is the one every commuter in Delhi has an opinion about. Who actually runs the city?</Person1>
<Person2>Delhi is its own special problem. It's a Union Territory, but Article 239AA gave it a Legislative Assembly back in 1991. The catch is that the Delhi Assembly cannot legislate on three subjects — public order, police, and land. Which is to say, on three of the things a city government most needs to actually govern a city.</Person2>
<Person1>And the rest — civil servants, the bureaucracy — was the subject of that big Constitution Bench verdict in May 2023.</Person1>
<Person2>The five-judge bench unanimously held that the elected Delhi government has power over "services" — meaning control over the IAS officers posted to Delhi. Civil servants answer to ministers, ministers answer to the Assembly, the Assembly answers to voters. A triple chain of accountability. It was, briefly, a famous win for the Delhi government.</Person2>
<Person1>Briefly being the operative word.</Person1>
<Person2>Parliament passed the GNCTD Amendment Act three months later, in August 2023, and effectively overrode the verdict. It created the National Capital Civil Services Authority. Three members — the Delhi CM chairs it, but the other two are the Chief Secretary and the Principal Home Secretary, both Union appointees. And the Lieutenant Governor has the final say on what the Authority recommends. So the elected CM is outvoted two-to-one on his own committee, and even then the LG can override.</Person2>
<Person1>That's a very Indian outcome. The court says one thing, Parliament says another, and the actual answer to "who runs Delhi" depends on which floor of which building you ask.</Person1>
<Person2>And that's the broader pattern. Scholars now talk about three flavours of federalism here. Co-operative — the GST Council, the NITI Aayog, states pooling sovereignty for shared gain. Competitive — states ranked on ease of doing business or vaccination coverage, fighting for central grants and private investment. And confrontational — which is most of what we see in the news. Governors sitting on bills, central agencies showing up in opposition-ruled states, language rows, GST compensation disputes.</Person2>
<Person1>And underneath all of that is the money question. The Finance Commission. Because federalism in India isn't really about who has the law-making power. It's about who has the cash.</Person1>
<Person2>That's the part I'd want every listener to take away. Every five years, a Finance Commission under Article 280 decides what share of central tax revenue goes to the states. The 14th Commission, under Y.V. Reddy, jumped the states' share from 32 to 42 percent — a genuine shift in fiscal federalism. The 15th brought it down by one point to 41, because J&K and Ladakh had become UTs. The 16th Commission's report was tabled in Parliament on February 1st this year, and it held the line at 41 percent for the next five years.</Person2>
<Person1>And the horizontal split — how that pie gets divided between states — is its own quiet political war. Population, income distance, area, demographic performance, forest cover.</Person1>
<Person2>Which is the same war that's coming for delimitation, by the way. The states that controlled their population growth get less. The states that didn't, get more. The Finance Commission formula is one version of that fight. The Lok Sabha seat allocation is the other version. They're going to collide some time in the next few years, and federalism in India is going to look very different on the other side of that collision.</Person2>
<Person1>So the open question isn't really about Article 1 or the Seventh Schedule. It's whether a Union built in 1950, with one state weighting from 1971 and a tax formula from 2021, can hold together when both those numbers finally get rewritten.</Person1>
