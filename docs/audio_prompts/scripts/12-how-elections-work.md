<Person1>Hi Aditya. I want to start with that purple ink on my left index finger. Because that mark is basically the last visible thing in a chain of about a million booths, fifteen million workers, and six weeks of choreography that almost nobody watches end-to-end.</Person1>
<Person2>Hi Neha. That's the right place to start, because the ink is the only part most of us ever see. The 2024 Lok Sabha (lohk SUB-hah) election ran in seven phases from April 19 to June 1, counting on June 4. Roughly 642 million votes cast out of 969 million eligible. To put that in scale — more people voted in India in 2024 than live in the European Union and the United States combined.</Person2>
<Person1>And the clock on all of this starts the moment the Election Commission tweets a date. That's literally the trigger.</Person1>
<Person2>The press conference, technically. The instant the ECI announces the schedule, the Model Code of Conduct kicks in. And the Model Code is a strange beast — it's not a statute, it's a consensual code the Commission enforces under Article 324 with what the legal scholars politely call "moral sanction." Which means the only real punishment is being publicly censured by the ECI.</Person2>
<Person1>So why does anyone obey it?</Person1>
<Person2>Because the ruling party also wants to be the ruling party in five years, and a censure on the front page is expensive. Also, some of the underlying acts — bribery, intimidation, impersonation, the "corrupt practices" under Section 123 of the Representation of the People Act — those are independently prosecutable. The Code itself is moral suasion. The crimes underneath it are not.</Person2>
<Person1>And the Code freezes the government in place. No new schemes, no transfers of officials, no ribbon-cuttings paid for by the public exchequer. For six weeks, a sitting government basically can't act like a government.</Person1>
<Person2>Which is the point. Then the calendar marches: notification, seven days of nominations, one day of scrutiny, two days for withdrawal, fourteen days minimum of campaigning, and then a forty-eight-hour silence period before the booth opens. Section 126 of the RPA. No rallies, no TV ads, no election content in those last forty-eight hours.</Person2>
<Person1>Which everyone knows is honoured mostly on television, because WhatsApp doesn't observe a silence period.</Person1>
<Person2>That's the gap the law hasn't caught up to. The Code was written when "broadcast" meant Doordarshan. A forwarded video at 11pm the night before polling is, technically, somebody's problem — but it isn't a problem the ECI has solved.</Person2>
<Person1>Let's talk about the machine the voter actually touches. The EVM. People my parents' age still don't fully trust it.</Person1>
<Person2>The history is instructive. EVMs were first used in May 1982 in fifty of one hundred and twenty-three booths of a single bye-election in Paravur, Kerala. The Supreme Court promptly struck that down in 1984, because the law at the time required paper ballots. Parliament had to amend the RPA in 1989 to legitimise the machine. They didn't come back into use until 1998, and the first fully-EVM general election was 2004.</Person2>
<Person1>So we romanticise the EVM as some uniquely Indian invention, but it took twenty-two years from prototype to nationwide rollout.</Person1>
<Person2>And another fifteen to be paired with a paper trail. The VVPAT — Voter-Verifiable Paper Audit Trail — was mandated by the Supreme Court in October 2013, in a petition filed by Subramanian Swamy. The Court said an election system without a verifiable record was constitutionally insufficient. By the 2019 general election, every EVM in the country was paired with one.</Person2>
<Person1>That's the slip I see for seven seconds in the little window before it drops into the box. Which always feels too short, by the way.</Person1>
<Person2>Seven seconds is the rule. And in 2019, in the Chandrababu Naidu case, the Supreme Court raised the mandatory cross-check from one polling station per Assembly Constituency to five. In April 2024, a fresh petition asked for one hundred percent VVPAT verification. The Court declined — but added a safeguard. If a losing candidate is within a small margin, they can request a re-verification of the EVM and VVPAT memory.</Person2>
<Person1>Five out of how many booths in a constituency, though? Because some assembly segments have hundreds.</Person1>
<Person2>That's the fair pushback. Five random booths out of, say, three hundred is a sample, not an audit. The Court's view is essentially a statistical bet — that if the machines were systematically rigged, five random checks would catch it. Critics say that's not how a constitutional right should work. The argument isn't settled.</Person2>
<Person1>And the booth itself has been quietly redesigned. In August 2024, the ECI raised the maximum electors per booth from twelve hundred to fifteen hundred. Which sounds technical until you're the person standing in line at 1pm in May.</Person1>
<Person2>That decision was challenged in the Supreme Court almost immediately, because the ECI also has a stated norm that no voter should travel more than two kilometres to a booth. Fewer booths means longer queues, longer waits, and arguably, fewer poor voters who can afford to wait. The Commission's defence was simpler — fewer booths means fewer Central Armed Police Forces needed to guard them, which is the binding constraint on how many phases an election takes.</Person2>
<Person1>That's why elections are phased. It's not philosophical. It's that the same paramilitary battalion physically moves from Bengal to Bihar to UP.</Person1>
<Person2>The CAPFs are the limiting reagent in Indian elections. Seven phases in 2024 wasn't a preference, it was a logistics constraint. If you had unlimited security, you could vote on one day.</Person2>
<Person1>Let's talk about the voter that the ECI is now trying to bring into the booth without the booth. Home voting for the elderly and persons with disabilities. That's new.</Person1>
<Person2>2024 was the first general election where it rolled out nationwide. If you're eighty-five or older, or a person with disability above a forty percent benchmark, you file Form 12D within five days of the notification, a polling team comes to your house with a ballot, and the whole thing is video-recorded. My grandmother voted from her bedroom in 2024. She was annoyed about it, actually — she'd voted in person since 1962.</Person2>
<Person1>Of course she was. The act of going to the booth was part of the citizenship for that generation.</Person1>
<Person2>It's a real loss, even if the policy is right. And it sits alongside the postal ballot system for armed forces and election-duty staff — the ETPBS, where a blank ballot is sent electronically, printed, marked, and physically returned. The idea is universal — nobody loses their vote because of where their job puts them.</Person2>
<Person1>And then there's NOTA. The "None of the Above" button. Which I confess I've never quite understood the point of.</Person1>
<Person2>It's almost entirely symbolic. The Supreme Court directed it in September 2013 in PUCL versus Union of India. The Court's reasoning was elegant — secrecy of the ballot should extend even to those who choose to vote for nobody, because otherwise their abstention itself becomes a political statement against them. But NOTA doesn't trigger a re-poll, even if it gets the highest share. The candidate with the most votes other than NOTA still wins.</Person2>
<Person1>So NOTA is a complaint card you file in private. It doesn't change the outcome.</Person1>
<Person2>At the national level, no. Some states — Maharashtra, Haryana, Puducherry, Delhi — have flirted with "right to reject" rules at the panchayat and municipal level. But for Lok Sabha and Assembly, NOTA is moral pressure on parties to nominate cleaner candidates. The empirical evidence that it works is, charitably, thin.</Person2>
<Person1>Speaking of clean candidates — let's talk about money. The ECI raised candidate expenditure limits in January 2022. Ninety-five lakh rupees for a Lok Sabha seat in a larger state. Which is a lot until you remember what an actual campaign costs.</Person1>
<Person2>Here's the loophole that swallows the rule. The cap is on the candidate. There is no statutory cap on what the party itself can spend. So the party flies the helicopter, the party prints the posters, the party runs the digital war room — and the candidate's ninety-five lakh covers, essentially, biryani and bus fare. The Centre for Media Studies estimated the 2024 Lok Sabha cost around a lakh crore rupees in total. The most expensive election in human history.</Person2>
<Person1>That's about twelve billion dollars to elect 543 people.</Person1>
<Person2>Which is the context for the Electoral Bonds story. The scheme was notified on January 2, 2018 — anonymous bearer bonds bought from SBI, deposited with any registered party, donor identity hidden from the public and from the recipient party officially. The 2018 amendment also removed the seven-and-a-half percent cap on corporate donations as a share of net profit.</Person2>
<Person1>And then in February 2024, the Supreme Court killed it.</Person1>
<Person2>Unanimously. A five-judge Constitution Bench led by Chief Justice Chandrachud held that anonymous political donations violated the voter's right to information under Article 19(1)(a). The Court also struck down the Companies Act amendment, restoring the seven-and-a-half percent cap. When the SBI data was finally published by the ECI in March 2024, the BJP was by far the largest beneficiary, followed by TMC, Congress, BRS. Top donors included Megha Engineering, Vedanta, Bharti Airtel, a company linked to the Adani group.</Person2>
<Person1>And the replacement is... what?</Person1>
<Person2>There isn't one. The ecosystem has reverted to electoral trusts and direct corporate giving, subject to the older disclosure regime. No successor transparency mechanism has been legislated. So the loophole isn't closed — it's just less anonymous than it was.</Person2>
<Person1>The last thing I want to get to is disqualification. Because Section 8 of the RPA is the rule that quietly decides who even gets to be on the ballot.</Person1>
<Person2>Section 8(3) is the one that matters most. Any conviction with a sentence of two years or more, and you're disqualified from the date of conviction, plus six years after release. For decades, sitting legislators got a three-month grace window under Section 8(4) to appeal without losing their seat. The Supreme Court struck that down in July 2013, in Lily Thomas versus Union of India. Now a sitting MP convicted of a two-year sentence is out immediately. That's the rule that disqualified Rahul Gandhi in 2023 after a defamation conviction — later stayed, but the mechanism worked exactly as the Court designed it.</Person2>
<Person1>So a single trial court verdict can remove an elected MP overnight. That's an enormous transfer of power from voters to judges.</Person1>
<Person2>It is. And it's the trade-off the Court made knowingly — the alternative was the older system where convicted legislators kept their seats for years on appeal. There's no clean answer here. Either elected politicians get special protection from criminal law, or the criminal courts can functionally overturn an election with a single ruling. Indian democracy chose the second.</Person2>
<Person1>Which is where it all comes back to the ECI itself, isn't it. The machinery that runs all of this is only as independent as the law lets it be. And right now the law is being rewritten — the CEC Act of 2023, the booth-size change, the Special Intensive Revision in West Bengal that's deleted around nine million names from the rolls ahead of the 2026 Assembly election.</Person1>
<Person2>The next decade of Indian elections will be argued out in those three rooms — who picks the Election Commissioners, how many voters fit in a booth, and who decides whose name belongs on the roll in the first place. The machinery I described is mostly settled. The question of who controls the machinery is wide open.</Person2>
