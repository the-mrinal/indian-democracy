<Person1>Hi Aditya. Today it's the money episode. And I want to start with a number that broke my brain a little — the 2024 Lok Sabha (lohk SUB-hah) election cost about one lakh crore rupees. The legal spending cap per candidate is ninety-five lakhs.</Person1>
<Person2>Hi Neha. That gap is basically the whole chapter. One lakh crore across the country, ninety-five lakh per candidate in a large state — and on paper, every single one of those 543 winners stayed within the limit. So one of those numbers is fiction. The interesting question is which fiction the system has decided to live with, and why.</Person2>
<Person1>Let's stay on the candidate cap for a second, because I think most people don't know what it even covers. If I'm contesting from, say, Lucknow, what am I actually allowed to spend ninety-five lakhs on?</Person1>
<Person2>Posters, banners, rallies, the loudspeaker van, the office rent, the boys you pay to put up flags, your own travel. The Election Commission revised the ceiling in January 2022 — ninety-five lakhs for a large-state Lok Sabha seat, seventy-five for a smaller state, forty for an assembly seat. You file a statement of expenses within thirty days of the result, and if you don't, Section 10A of the Representation of the People Act can disqualify you for three years.</Person2>
<Person1>Three years sounds serious. But you said the number is a fiction — so does anyone actually get caught?</Person1>
<Person2>Almost never on the cap itself. People get disqualified for not filing the return, which is a paperwork offence. Getting nailed for actually spending more is extraordinarily rare, because the cap has two doors built into it that you can drive a truck through.</Person2>
<Person1>Walk me through the doors.</Person1>
<Person2>Door one: star campaigner travel doesn't count against the candidate. So when the Prime Minister or the party president flies into your constituency in a chartered helicopter and addresses a rally for you, that bill — fuel, security, stage, sound — sits on the party's books, not yours. Door two is bigger. There is no statutory cap at all on what the political party itself can spend. Zero. The candidate is on a leash; the party is off-leash entirely.</Person2>
<Person1>So the ninety-five lakh limit only governs the candidate's personal campaign, and everything that flows in through the party balloon is unmetered.</Person1>
<Person2>Correct. And then sitting underneath both of those is the cash economy — the envelope politics that people like Shivam Shankar Singh and Milan Vaishnav (mee-LAHN vy-sh-NAV) write about openly. Liquor, sarees, currency notes in the last forty-eight hours. The ECI's flying squads seized thousands of crores in cash in 2024 alone, and that's only what they caught.</Person2>
<Person1>I want to push back a little here. Are we sure cash distribution actually moves votes? I've stood in those queues at seven in the morning, and a lot of people in line genuinely don't decide based on what someone handed them the night before.</Person1>
<Person2>Fair qualification. The honest answer is that nobody fully knows. The practitioner accounts say it tips marginal voters and turnout in tight seats. But Vaishnav's own work suggests caste arithmetic and candidate identity matter more than the envelope. The envelope might be necessary without being sufficient — you have to pay to play, even if paying doesn't guarantee you win.</Person2>
<Person1>Okay. Let's move to the part of the story where the government tried to clean some of this up, or at least said it was trying. Electoral Bonds. Arun Jaitley announced them in the 2017 budget, notified in January 2018. What were they?</Person1>
<Person2>A donor — usually a company — could walk into the State Bank of India, buy a bearer bond in denominations from a thousand rupees up to a crore, and hand it to any party that had crossed one percent of the vote in the last election. The party deposited it, encashed it within fifteen days. The donor's name was hidden from the public, from the Election Commission, and from the receiving party.</Person2>
<Person1>Hidden from the receiving party — is that real, though? If Adani's people walk into your office and tell you a bond is coming, you know who it's from.</Person1>
<Person2>That's exactly the critique. On paper, anonymous. In practice, the donor tells you. And the one person who definitely knew was the government, because SBI is a public sector bank that the finance ministry oversees. So you had a regime where the public was blind, the ECI was blind, but the ruling party could in principle find out who was funding the opposition. That asymmetry was the core constitutional problem.</Person2>
<Person1>And the 2018 scheme also did something to the corporate donation rules that I think most people missed at the time.</Person1>
<Person2>It quietly took the lid off. Under Section 182 of the Companies Act, a company couldn't donate more than seven and a half percent of its average net profit over three years to political parties, and it had to disclose the party-wise breakdown in its annual report. The 2017 Finance Act removed both — the cap and the disclosure. Which meant a shell company with no real business, no profits, could in theory route unlimited money through a bond. That is a money-laundering structure, not a transparency reform.</Person2>
<Person1>And the Supreme Court eventually said all of that out loud.</Person1>
<Person2>Fifteenth February 2024. A five-judge Constitution Bench led by Chief Justice Chandrachud (chund-rah-CHOOD), unanimous. They struck down the bond scheme itself, the removal of the seven-and-a-half percent cap, and the income tax amendment that allowed non-disclosure. The reasoning was Article 19(1)(a) — the voter's right to know who funds the people asking for their vote is part of the right to free expression.</Person2>
<Person1>And then there was that follow-up moment where SBI tried to drag its feet on actually publishing the data.</Person1>
<Person2>SBI asked for an extension till the end of June, conveniently after the 2024 election. The Court was not amused. On the eleventh of March it told SBI to hand everything over by the next day's close of business. The ECI put the data online on the fifteenth of March. We finally got to see who had been buying what.</Person2>
<Person1>And the headline from that data?</Person1>
<Person2>The BJP received roughly forty-seven percent of all bond value — somewhere around six thousand crores. TMC, Congress, BRS, BJD, DMK, AAP, AIADMK, JD(S) also received bonds, but no one was close. On the buyer side, names like Megha Engineering, Vedanta, Bharti Airtel, a Quick Supply Chain entity linked to the Adani group, Aurobindo Pharma, Reliance. Several of those companies had ongoing enforcement actions when they bought the bonds. The pattern is suggestive; proving quid pro quo in court is a different matter.</Person2>
<Person1>So the Court struck it down. What replaced it?</Person1>
<Person2>Nothing legislated. We've quietly slid back to Electoral Trusts, direct corporate donations now subject to the restored cap, and cash. The Indrajit Gupta Committee in 1998 and the Law Commission in 2015 both proposed state funding of elections. The Election Commission itself, in 2004 and 2016, proposed a National Election Fund — companies donate to a common pool, the pool distributes based on vote share. None of it has been enacted. Which is itself a kind of answer.</Person2>
<Person1>Okay. Let's switch tracks to the other half of the chapter, which I find more uncomfortable. Milan Vaishnav's When Crime Pays. The 18th Lok Sabha, elected in June 2024 — forty-six percent of MPs have declared criminal cases against them.</Person1>
<Person2>Two hundred and fifty-one out of five hundred and forty-three. A record high. And one hundred and seventy of them face what the ADR classifies as serious cases — murder, attempt to murder, kidnapping, crimes against women. Twenty-seven were already convicted when they took their oath. Since 2009, the number of MPs with criminal cases is up fifty-five percent, and the serious-case count is up a hundred and twenty-four.</Person2>
<Person1>The intuitive reaction is: voters must not know. If they knew, they wouldn't vote for these candidates.</Person1>
<Person2>That is exactly the framing Vaishnav blows up. Since the 2002 and 2003 ADR judgments, candidates have been legally required to disclose criminal record, assets, education in an affidavit. Since the 2018 Public Interest Foundation ruling, parties have to publish the criminal antecedents of their candidates in newspapers, on their websites, on social media. The information is out there. ADR has been compiling it for two decades. Voters know, and they vote them in anyway. ADR's 2024 data is brutal on this point — candidates with criminal cases had significantly higher win rates than clean candidates.</Person2>
<Person1>So why? That's the part I find hardest to sit with.</Person1>
<Person2>Vaishnav's answer is that we're moralising a problem that's actually structural. Where the state genuinely delivers — police arrive, schools function, courts decide in reasonable time — voters don't need a strongman. Where the state is absent or hostile, a candidate with a reputation for getting things done, dispensing rough justice, protecting his community's interests, becomes a rational pick. A criminal record can read as a credential. It's the demand side of the market, not just the supply side.</Person2>
<Person1>And the party side?</Person1>
<Person2>Parties love these candidates for two reasons. One, they are self-financing — they bring their own money and reduce the demand on the party kitty, which loops us right back to the first half of this conversation. Two, they win. So if you're a party general secretary picking forty candidates for your state, you can either do the morally clean thing and lose, or pick the winnable candidate and form the government. Most parties pick the second option, most of the time.</Person2>
<Person1>The Supreme Court has tried to intervene more than once, though.</Person1>
<Person2>Repeatedly. ADR in 2002 and 2003 forced disclosure. Lily Thomas in 2013 struck down Section 8(4) of the RPA — so any sitting MP or MLA convicted of an offence carrying two years or more is disqualified immediately, no grace period for appeals. That's the provision under which Rahul Gandhi briefly lost his seat in 2023. Public Interest Foundation in 2018, the Brajesh Singh follow-ups in 2021 and 2022 — all pushing publication and contempt for non-compliance. None of it has shifted candidate selection in any meaningful way.</Person2>
<Person1>Because the underlying incentive — winnability — hasn't moved.</Person1>
<Person2>That's the whole argument. Disclosure assumes voters punish criminality. The data says voters often reward it, or at minimum don't punish it enough to matter. Until that calculus changes — which means until state delivery improves enough that voters stop needing a strongman — the share of MPs with cases is going to keep climbing.</Person2>
<Person1>So we end up with a system where the financing is opaque, the candidates are heavily criminalised, and the law-and-policy responses from both Court and Commission have nibbled at the edges without solving any of it.</Person1>
