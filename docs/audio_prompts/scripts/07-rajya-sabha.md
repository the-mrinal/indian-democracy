<Person1>Hi Aditya. Today it's the other house — the Rajya Sabha (RAHJ-yuh SUB-hah). And I want to start with something that bothered me the first time I learned it: I have never voted for a Rajya Sabha MP in my life. Neither has anyone I know.</Person1>
<Person2>Hi Neha. And you never will, unless you become an MLA. That's the whole design. The Rajya Sabha was never meant to be elected by you and me. It's the Council of States — 245 members, chosen mostly by state legislators, sitting in a chamber that is never, ever dissolved. The Lok Sabha is the people's voice. This is the states' voice. Two completely different theories of representation sharing the same building.</Person2>
<Person1>And the indirect election is what trips most people up. Walk me through it — if a Rajya Sabha seat opens up from, say, Karnataka, who actually votes?</Person1>
<Person2>The elected MLAs of the Karnataka assembly. Only them. Not the governor's nominees, not MPs, not the public. And they don't vote with a simple show of hands either — they use a Single Transferable Vote system under proportional representation. Each MLA ranks the candidates in order of preference. There's a quota a candidate has to hit to win, calculated using something called the Droop quota — roughly, total votes divided by seats-plus-one, plus one. Surplus votes above the quota get transferred to next preferences. Lowest-scoring candidates get eliminated, their votes transferred. It keeps going until every seat is filled.</Person2>
<Person1>So it's deliberately designed so that a party with sixty percent of the assembly can't sweep all the Rajya Sabha seats from that state.</Person1>
<Person2>That's the whole point. A regional party with twenty percent of MLAs can still send someone up. It mirrors the actual political composition of the state, not just the winner. Which is why even when one party dominates the Lok Sabha nationally, the Rajya Sabha can look very different — regional parties hang on through their MLAs.</Person2>
<Person1>Okay, but I want to push on something here. If this is the Council of States, why is a Gujarati industrialist contesting from Karnataka? That's been happening for years. Doesn't that defeat the entire federal idea?</Person1>
<Person2>It does, and that's the most controversial thing this chamber has done to itself. Until 2003, you had to be ordinarily resident in the state you were contesting from. The Representation of the People Amendment Act in 2003 quietly removed that requirement. So now, technically, a person from Delhi can contest from Madhya Pradesh as long as some MLAs in MP are willing to vote for them.</Person2>
<Person1>That's a big deal. Was there pushback?</Person1>
<Person2>There was. It went to the Supreme Court in Kuldip Nayar versus Union of India in 2006. The court upheld it. Their reasoning was essentially that "state representation" doesn't require residence — being chosen by the state's MLAs is enough of a link. Critics still call it a polite fiction. If a state is sending up someone who's never lived there, then what exactly is being represented?</Person2>
<Person1>The same 2003 amendment also changed how MLAs vote, right? It used to be a secret ballot.</Person1>
<Person2>It did, and that change is arguably more consequential. Cross-voting had become an industry — MLAs would take the party whip, then vote for someone else in the secret ballot, money would change hands, nobody could prove a thing. So the 2003 amendment made it an open ballot. The MLA has to show their marked ballot to the authorised representative of their own party before dropping it in the box.</Person2>
<Person1>Which sounds reasonable until you remember that the entire point of a secret ballot is that nobody can punish you for how you voted.</Person1>
<Person2>That's the tension. You've traded one problem — corruption — for another — coercion. An MLA who genuinely disagrees with their party's nominee now has no privacy. The Supreme Court upheld this too, in the same Kuldip Nayar case. Their view was that party discipline matters more than individual MLA conscience when you're choosing the upper house. You can decide for yourself whether that's a comfortable answer.</Person2>
<Person1>Let's talk about the thing that makes the Rajya Sabha truly different from the Lok Sabha — it never dies. Even after a general election sweeps everything out, the Rajya Sabha just keeps going.</Person1>
<Person2>That's the most underrated feature of the whole institution. Members serve six-year terms, and one-third of them retire every two years. The math is deliberate. It means no single election, no matter how big the wave, can flip the composition of the Rajya Sabha overnight. You'd need three consecutive cycles — six years of sustained dominance across multiple state assemblies — to truly reshape it. It's a counter-majoritarian buffer, built into the calendar.</Person2>
<Person1>Has that buffer mattered in practice? Or is it just constitutional theory?</Person1>
<Person2>It has mattered enormously. Think about the first Modi government, 2014 to 2019. The BJP had a thumping Lok Sabha majority — over 280 seats. But for most of that term they didn't control the Rajya Sabha. Which is why bills like the GST took years of negotiation, why land acquisition reform died, why the triple talaq bill kept bouncing back. The upper house slowed the train down. Whether that's a feature or a bug depends on which side of the bill you were on.</Person2>
<Person1>So Parliament can have, in effect, two different political colours at the same time.</Person1>
<Person2>Two different majorities reflecting two different moments. The Lok Sabha reflects this week's mood. The Rajya Sabha reflects the last six years of state-level politics. That's the federal counterweight working exactly as designed.</Person2>
<Person1>Now what about the things only the Rajya Sabha can do? There must be some powers the Lok Sabha doesn't have, otherwise why bother with this chamber at all.</Person1>
<Person2>There are exactly two, and they're both about federalism. Article 249 — if the Rajya Sabha passes a resolution by two-thirds of members present, declaring that something in the State List needs national legislation, Parliament can legislate on it for up to a year. Renewable. The logic is that since the Rajya Sabha represents the states, only it can give the states' consent to having their turf encroached on. The other one is Article 312 — if you want to create a new All-India Service beyond the IAS, IPS, and Indian Forest Service, you need a Rajya Sabha resolution with the same two-thirds majority.</Person2>
<Person1>That's a much shorter list than I expected.</Person1>
<Person2>It is. And the asymmetry the other way is much sharper. Money Bills can only originate in the Lok Sabha. Demands for Grants — that's the actual budget approval — only the Lok Sabha votes on them; the Rajya Sabha can discuss but not vote. And the big one: no-confidence motions can only be moved in the Lok Sabha. The government does not answer to the Rajya Sabha. It can't be brought down there. Which is exactly why the Prime Minister has to sit in the Lok Sabha, or at least be answerable to it.</Person2>
<Person1>The Money Bill thing has a specific timeline though. The Rajya Sabha gets a window to look at it.</Person1>
<Person2>Fourteen days. That's it. A Money Bill goes from the Lok Sabha to the Rajya Sabha, and the upper house has fourteen days to return it with or without recommendations. The Lok Sabha is free to accept or ignore those recommendations. And if the Rajya Sabha just sits on it past fourteen days, the bill is deemed passed. No deadlock possible. That's why the Speaker's power to classify what counts as a Money Bill is so politically charged — once that label is on, the Rajya Sabha is essentially bypassed.</Person2>
<Person1>And there's no joint sitting option to break a deadlock on Money Bills either.</Person1>
<Person2>None. The joint sitting under Article 108 exists for ordinary bills and financial bills that aren't Money Bills. It's been used precisely three times in the entire history of independent India — the Dowry Prohibition Bill in 1961, a banking commission bill in 1978, and the Prevention of Terrorism Bill in 2002. That's it. Three times in seventy-five years. And even that mechanism can't be used for constitutional amendments — each house has to pass those independently with a special majority.</Person2>
<Person1>Let's talk about the twelve nominated members. The President picks them — literature, science, art, social service. Sachin Tendulkar was one, Shabana Azmi, Ruskin Bond. How does that fit into a chamber that's supposed to represent states?</Person1>
<Person2>It doesn't, really. It's a graft from the Irish Constitution. The idea was that Parliament shouldn't be deprived of voices that can't or won't fight elections — scientists, artists, jurists, people like M.S. Swaminathan or L.M. Singhvi. In practice, of course, governments have used those twelve seats for political loyalists too. The convention is one thing; the use is another. They get a vote in the Vice-Presidential election but not the Presidential one, which is a quirk worth noting.</Person2>
<Person1>Speaking of the Vice-President — he's the Chairperson of the Rajya Sabha but he's not actually a member of it.</Person1>
<Person2>Right. The Vice-President of India is ex officio Chairperson under Article 64. He presides, he rules on points of order, but he doesn't get a vote unless there's a tie. The current Chairperson is C.P. Radhakrishnan, the fifteenth VP, who took over in September 2025 after Jagdeep Dhankhar's resignation. The Deputy Chairperson, on the other hand, is elected by the Rajya Sabha from amongst its own members. So you have an outsider presiding and an insider as deputy. That arrangement is itself a federal compromise — the VP is elected by an electoral college of both Houses, so he's seen as standing above the chamber.</Person2>
<Person1>Last thing — the seat distribution. UP gets 31, the small states get one each. Sikkim, Goa, Nagaland — one seat. That doesn't feel like equal state representation the way, say, the US Senate has two senators per state regardless of size.</Person1>
<Person2>It isn't. And that's a deliberate choice the Constituent Assembly made. The American model gives Wyoming the same Senate power as California. India chose a population-weighted upper house — UP has thirty-one seats, Maharashtra has nineteen, Tamil Nadu has eighteen, and Sikkim has one. The reasoning was that giving tiny states equal weight in a country this large would create permanent gridlock. So the Rajya Sabha is federal in spirit, but proportional in arithmetic. A halfway house.</Person2>
<Person1>Which means the same delimitation question that haunts the Lok Sabha eventually comes for this house too — if state populations keep diverging, the South's share of the Rajya Sabha shrinks just as much as it does in the Lok Sabha.</Person1>
<Person2>That's the conversation nobody's having yet. Everyone's focused on the Lok Sabha redrawing because that's where governments are made. But the federal counterweight is built on the same demographic foundation. If the foundation tilts, both houses tilt the same way. And then the question becomes — what is left in the architecture to actually speak for the states?</Person2>
