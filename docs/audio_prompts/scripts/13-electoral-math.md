<Person1>Hi Aditya. I want to start with a number that makes no sense on the surface — BJP got about 37 percent of the national vote in 2019 and ended up with 303 Lok Sabha (lohk SUB-hah) seats. That's a clean majority off a vote share that wouldn't get you elected class monitor.</Person1>
<Person2>Hi Neha. And that's the whole chapter in one number, honestly. India doesn't run a national election. It runs 543 separate FPTP — First-Past-The-Post — contests on the same day, and we add them up and pretend it's one race. The "national vote share" is a journalist's convenience. It doesn't decide a single seat. The rule has one line. In each constituency, the person with the most votes wins. Not the most votes above 50 percent — the most votes, full stop. If three candidates split 34-33-33, the 34 wins. Everything else you've ever heard about Indian elections is a consequence of that one sentence.</Person2>
<Person1>So when my uncle texts the family group saying "BJP only got 37 percent, they don't have a mandate" — he's technically right about the percentage and completely wrong about how the system works.</Person1>
<Person2>Tell your uncle to look at BSP in 2014. Over 4 percent of the national vote — tens of millions of voters standing in queues at seven in the morning — and zero seats. Vote spread like butter across UP. Came second in lots of places. Second in FPTP is just an expensive way of losing. Now compare DMK in 2024 — five or six percent nationally, but concentrated in Tamil Nadu, and they walked out with around twenty-two seats. Same math, opposite outcome. Regional concentration wins. Dispersed vote wastes.</Person2>
<Person1>And then the other side of that coin — Congress in 2014. Nineteen-point-three percent of the national vote and only forty-four seats. Worst LS performance in their history. The UPA total didn't even cross the fifty-five seats you need to claim Leader of the Opposition. A national party with a fifth of the country's votes, and no recognised LOP for five whole years.</Person1>
<Person2>That's FPTP doing what FPTP does. It magnifies pluralities into majorities and wastes everything that doesn't help elect a winner. Though I'd push back gently on the word "wasted" — it's a technical term, not a moral one. A losing vote still tells the winner how vulnerable he is, keeps a second party alive on the ground for next time. The chapter's point is narrower. The aggregate national vote share number — the one TV anchors put on screen — doesn't mean what people think it means. There's no national race. Just 543 little knife-fights.</Person2>
<Person1>Okay. So FPTP runs the whole visible democracy — Lok Sabha, all the State Assemblies, panchayats, municipal wards. The button I press on the EVM, every time, is FPTP. But there's a second electoral system running quietly in parallel that nobody I know has ever voted in.</Person1>
<Person2>Because you have to be a legislator to do it. Single Transferable Vote with Proportional Representation. STV-PR. That's how the Rajya Sabha gets elected, the Legislative Councils in the six states that have them, the President, the Vice-President. Same country, same democracy, two completely different theories of representation operating side by side.</Person2>
<Person1>Walk me through it, because every time someone tries to explain Rajya Sabha elections on TV I tune out around the word "quota."</Person1>
<Person2>The quota is actually the cleanest part. Take a state with five Rajya Sabha seats to fill and 120 MLAs voting. The Droop quota is total votes divided by seats-plus-one, plus one. So 120 over 6, plus 1, equals 21. Any candidate who gets 21 first-preference votes from MLAs is in — elected. And MLAs aren't ticking one box, they're ranking — one, two, three. If someone gets 35 first-preferences, they only needed 21, so the surplus 14 votes transfer to whoever those MLAs ranked second, at a reduced "transfer value" so the math doesn't double-count. If nobody hits quota, the bottom candidate is eliminated and their second-preferences flow up. You iterate till all five seats are full.</Person2>
<Person1>So if a party has 60 percent of the MLAs, it gets roughly three out of five seats. Seat share tracks vote share almost exactly. The opposite of what FPTP does. Which is the point — the Rajya Sabha is supposed to mirror the state-by-state political landscape proportionally, the way the Lok Sabha very deliberately does not mirror the national one. Two different theories of representation, sitting in two chambers about a hundred metres apart in the same building. And then the President's election is STV-PR with extra steps — the weighted votes thing. Walk me through that, because this is where I always lose the thread.</Person1>
<Person2>The Electoral College is all MPs plus all MLAs of all states. But an MLA from UP doesn't have the same vote weight as an MLA from Sikkim, because UP has many more people. The formula divides the state's 1971 population by its number of elected MLAs, then divides by a thousand. For UP, about 208 vote units per MLA. For Tamil Nadu, about 176. For Sikkim, about 7. Every MP gets a vote value worth 700 of those units, so the MP pool and the MLA pool come out roughly balanced. In 2022, Murmu won by about 296,000 vote-value units out of a total pool of just over ten lakh.</Person2>
<Person1>Hold on. 1971 population. We're electing the President in 2022, in 2027, and we're using a census from when my parents were teenagers.</Person1>
<Person2>Frozen by the 42nd Amendment in 1976. Extended by the 84th Amendment in 2001, all the way out to "the first census after 2026." So yes — Droupadi Murmu was elected in 2022 using a population snapshot taken before the Emergency, before liberalisation, before the smartphone. Officially the freeze was to reward states that did family planning well, mostly southern ones. Unofficially it was a peace treaty. Without it, the South would have started losing parliamentary weight decades ago, and we'd have had a very different federation by now.</Person2>
<Person1>And that peace treaty is about to expire. The first post-2026 census is scheduled for March 2027, and the whole political class knows what comes next.</Person1>
<Person2>If seats get reallocated on 2027 population, UP gains, Bihar gains, MP and Rajasthan gain. Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Karnataka, Andhra — they all lose proportional share. The government already tried to move early on this. The 131st Amendment Bill, introduced 16 April 2026 — defeated 298 to 230, well short of the two-thirds majority you need to change the Constitution. That vote, which barely made the front page next to whatever cricket thing was happening that week, was actually the South successfully blocking the redrawing of the federation. For now. And it's tangled up with women's reservation, because that's hostage to the same census.</Person2>
<Person1>The Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam — 106th Amendment, 2023. One-third of seats for women in the Lok Sabha and the State Assemblies. Passed 454 to 2. Notified into force in April 2026. Hasn't applied to a single election yet, because Article 334A says it kicks in only after a delimitation based on the first census after the law was notified. Earliest realistic application is the 2029 Lok Sabha election. Could slip further. And it rotates — different constituencies become women-reserved in different cycles, which means a sitting MP can wake up to find his seat reserved out from under him for the next election. Plus the SC and ST math, which we should mention — Articles 330 and 332. Eighty-four SC seats, forty-seven ST seats. How does that work on voting day?</Person1>
<Person2>If your constituency is SC-reserved, only an SC candidate can stand. But every voter in the constituency — every caste, every community — votes in that election. Reserved candidate pool, not a reserved electorate. Extended till 2030 by the 104th Amendment. Same amendment that finally abolished the two nominated Anglo-Indian seats, which is why the House is now 543 and not 545.</Person2>
<Person1>The last piece of math I want to end on — party loyalty. The Tenth Schedule, anti-defection. This is in the headlines every other week.</Person1>
<Person2>52nd Amendment, 1985. Sounds simple — if you "voluntarily give up" your party membership, or vote against a party whip without permission, you can be disqualified. The Speaker decides. The original law had a loophole — if one-third of your party split off together, you were exempt. The 91st Amendment in 2003 closed that. Now you need two-thirds to merge with another party. Anything less and you're out.</Person2>
<Person1>Except the Speaker decides, and the Speaker is almost always from the ruling side.</Person1>
<Person2>Which is the polite phrasing. The chapter cites two 2024 cases — the Shiv Sena split, where the Speaker ruled the Shinde faction was the "real" Shiv Sena in January, and the NCP split, where the Speaker ruled the Ajit Pawar faction was the "real" NCP in February. Two-thirds threshold somehow met in both cases, somehow ratified by the Speaker, somehow conveniently for the government of the day. The Supreme Court tried to discipline this in the Manipur case in 2020 — said Speakers should decide within three months. "Suggested." Not statutorily binding. Which in practice means a Speaker can sit on a defection petition for as long as the political calendar requires.</Person2>
<Person1>And there are three perennial reform ideas floating around all of this that the chapter flags — replacing FPTP with proportional representation, the Law Commission floated it back in 1999. A right to recall your MP mid-term, which actually exists at panchayat level in MP, Chhattisgarh, Bihar. And One Nation One Election, which got a whole Kovind Committee report in March 2024 and a Constitution Amendment Bill tabled that December.</Person1>
<Person2>None of which have moved much. PR would weaken single-party dominance and weaken the geographic accountability of your MP being your MP — two things parties on both sides quietly value. Right to Recall at the Lok Sabha level is a non-starter; no sitting MP wants to vote for it. And One Nation One Election runs straight into the constitutional question of what happens when a state assembly falls mid-cycle. Reform ideas in India have a long shelf life and a very short legislative one.</Person2>
<Person1>So if I'm summarising the whole chapter in one thought — India runs a visible election that magnifies pluralities into majorities, an invisible election that mirrors them proportionally, a presidential election anchored in a 1971 census, and a defection law that depends entirely on who's holding the gavel.</Person1>
<Person2>And every single one of those four things is up for renegotiation in the next five years. The 2027 census. The post-census delimitation. Whether women's reservation actually arrives in 2029 or slides to 2034. Whether the South accepts losing seats, or whether we end up with some compromise nobody's drafted yet. The math is settled. The politics of the math is wide open.</Person2>
<Person1>And the people deciding all of that were elected under the version of the rules that the new rules would replace.</Person1>
