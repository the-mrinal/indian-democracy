<Person1>Morning Aditya. Today, the Constitution. And I want to start with a number that genuinely shocked me when I looked it up — 145,000 words. The longest written constitution in the world. Why on earth did India need a document that long?</Person1>
<Person2>Morning Neha. Because in 1946 India was being asked to do something nobody had attempted at that scale — write a single rulebook for a country that included princely Mysore, tribal Assam, urban Bombay, and a few hundred million mostly illiterate citizens, and have it all start working on day one. The American framers could afford to be terse because they were ten thousand miles from any reviewing court and writing for thirteen broadly similar colonies. The Constituent Assembly was writing for a subcontinent. Brevity was a luxury they did not have.</Person2>
<Person1>So let's set the scene. The Assembly first sits on 9 December 1946. Who's actually in the room?</Person1>
<Person2>389 members at full strength — 292 from British Indian provinces, 93 from the princely states, four from chief commissioner provinces. After Partition in August 1947 it shrinks to 299. Rajendra Prasad presides. Nehru (NEH-roo) moves the Objectives Resolution on 13 December — that's the philosophical seed that becomes the Preamble. Sardar Patel chairs the committee on Fundamental Rights. Sir B.N. Rau, the constitutional adviser, hands them an initial draft. And then Ambedkar, as chairman of the seven-member Drafting Committee, does the actual hammering for the next two years, eleven months, and eighteen days.</Person2>
<Person1>That precise count — two years, eleven months, eighteen days — is one of those things every school textbook drills into you. But what were they actually arguing about for nearly three years?</Person1>
<Person2>Everything. Should the official language be Hindi or stay English. Should there be separate electorates for minorities — Patel killed that one. Should property be a fundamental right. Should the President be a real executive or a ceremonial head. Whether to call the country India, Bharat, or both. They held 167 days of plenary debate. And the transcripts are remarkably civil for people who disagreed on almost everything.</Person2>
<Person1>And the date they pick to bring it into force — 26 January 1950 — that's not random.</Person1>
<Person2>Not at all. On 26 January 1930, the Lahore Session of Congress had declared Purna Swaraj (POOR-nuh sva-RAJ) — complete independence. So when the Assembly adopted the document on 26 November 1949 but waited two months to commence it, they were deliberately tying the new Republic back to that 1930 pledge. The Constitution doesn't begin on Independence Day, on 15 August. It begins on the anniversary of the demand for independence. It's a small choice that tells you how seriously they took symbolic continuity.</Person2>
<Person1>I want to push on something though. Ambedkar himself said he had "ransacked all the known Constitutions of the world." Critics sometimes wave that around to say the document isn't really Indian — it's a borrowed thing.</Person1>
<Person2>That criticism is lazy. Yes, parliamentary government and single citizenship came from the UK, fundamental rights and judicial review from the US, directive principles from Ireland, the strong-centre federation from Canada, emergency provisions from Weimar Germany. But the most direct ancestor of the Indian Constitution is not foreign at all — it's the Government of India Act, 1935. Roughly two-thirds of the administrative grammar, the Governor's office, the public service commissions, even the emergency framework, all come from a colonial statute the British wrote to run India more efficiently. The Assembly inherited the plumbing and then decided what kind of house to build on top of it.</Person2>
<Person1>That's an uncomfortable inheritance, isn't it? Independence Constitution built on a colonial Act.</Person1>
<Person2>It's uncomfortable, but it's why the thing worked from day one. They were not designing on a blank sheet. The bureaucracy, the high courts, the railways, the post office, the districts — all of that existed and was already functioning under the 1935 Act. The Constitution adapts what works and replaces what doesn't. Pragmatic, not romantic.</Person2>
<Person1>Okay. The document itself. Originally 395 Articles in 22 Parts and 8 Schedules. Today, after 106 amendments, we're at roughly 448 Articles, 25 Parts, 12 Schedules. That averages out to one amendment roughly every eight months for 75 years.</Person1>
<Person2>Compare the United States — 27 amendments in 235 years, one every nine years. We amend ours thirty times more often. Which sounds alarming until you remember the US can pass an ordinary law to fix what we'd need a constitutional amendment to fix, because their document is short and ours specifies things down to the salaries of judges in the Second Schedule.</Person2>
<Person1>So a lot of our amendments aren't dramatic — they're housekeeping.</Person1>
<Person2>Exactly the right way to read it. The First Schedule lists every state and union territory by territorial extent — every time you create Telangana out of Andhra Pradesh, that's a constitutional amendment. The Eighth Schedule lists official languages — when Bodo, Dogri, Maithili and Santhali were added in 2003, that was the 92nd Amendment. A lot of the 106 are administrative edits, not philosophical rewrites.</Person2>
<Person1>Let's talk about the Preamble. Because it's the part everyone has read — at least the kids who had to recite it on Republic Day. "We the people of India, having solemnly resolved..." Sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic, republic. Justice, liberty, equality, fraternity.</Person1>
<Person2>And here's the thing most people don't know — two of those words weren't in the original. "Socialist" and "Secular" were inserted by the 42nd Amendment in December 1976, during the Emergency, by the Indira Gandhi government. The original Preamble just read "Sovereign Democratic Republic." The same amendment also changed "unity of the Nation" to "unity and integrity of the Nation."</Person2>
<Person1>So the Preamble we recite is not the one the Constituent Assembly adopted. That feels significant.</Person1>
<Person2>It is, and it remains contested. In 2024, in Dr. Balram Singh v. Union of India, the Supreme Court upheld those insertions — held that "socialist" and "secular" were consistent with the existing ideals of the document and didn't violate the Basic Structure. But politically, you'll still hear arguments that an amendment passed during the Emergency, when most opposition leaders were in jail, shouldn't have the same legitimacy as the words 299 members debated in 1949.</Person2>
<Person1>Which brings us to Basic Structure. This is the doctrine that I think every educated Indian has heard mentioned and almost nobody can actually explain. What is it?</Person1>
<Person2>The cleanest way to put it — Article 368 lets Parliament amend the Constitution. The question Kesavananda Bharati (kay-sha-vuh-NUN-duh BHAR-uh-tee) asked in 1973 was, can Parliament amend it out of existence? Can a Parliament with a two-thirds majority, for example, abolish judicial review, extend its own tenure indefinitely, or get rid of elections? A 13-judge bench by a 7-to-6 majority — the thinnest margin you can have on a 13-judge bench — said no. Parliament can amend anything, but it cannot destroy the Constitution's basic structure.</Person2>
<Person1>7-to-6. One judge.</Person1>
<Person2>One judge. The single most consequential vote in Indian constitutional history was cast by Justice H.R. Khanna, who broke ranks with what was expected to be a 6-7 the other way. And here's the irony — Basic Structure is itself judge-made. It's nowhere in the text. The Court essentially invented a non-textual ceiling on Parliament's textual amendment power. Minerva Mills in 1980 reinforced it. I.R. Coelho in 2007 extended it to the Ninth Schedule.</Person2>
<Person1>So when somebody says "the Basic Structure protects democracy" — what's actually on the list?</Person1>
<Person2>The Court hasn't given a closed list, which is partly the point — it lets future benches add to it. But over fifty years they've identified supremacy of the Constitution, secularism, federalism, democracy, the republican form of government, rule of law, separation of powers, free and fair elections, judicial review, and the dignity of the individual. If a future government with 367 Lok Sabha seats wanted to, say, postpone elections indefinitely, the Supreme Court could strike that down even though Parliament passed it by the required majority.</Person2>
<Person1>That's a lot of trust to put in thirteen judges sitting in Delhi.</Person1>
<Person2>It is. And every few years somebody — usually whoever is in power — points out that the Basic Structure doctrine has no textual basis and that judges are not elected. They're not wrong. But the alternative is letting a temporary supermajority do permanent damage, and that risk has felt more pressing than the risk of judicial overreach, at least so far.</Person2>
<Person1>Let's get practical. How do you actually amend the Constitution? Because not everything needs the same procedure.</Person1>
<Person2>Three flavours. Simple majority — for things like admitting a new state, you just need an ordinary law. Special majority — majority of total membership of each House plus at least two-thirds of those present and voting — that covers most amendments, Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles, all of that. And then the heaviest mode — special majority plus ratification by at least half the state legislatures. That's for federal provisions: presidential election, the Supreme Court, the Seventh Schedule lists, and Article 368 itself.</Person2>
<Person1>So changing how the President is elected requires not just two-thirds of Parliament but half the states actively voting yes. That's a high bar.</Person1>
<Person2>Deliberately. Anything that shifts the federal balance can't be done by the Union alone. Which incidentally is why the GST amendment — the 101st in 2016 — was such a major piece of constitutional engineering. It rewrote how the Centre and states share tax powers, and required exactly that highest-level ratification.</Person2>
<Person1>Let me ask you about the women's reservation. The 106th Amendment in 2023 — Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam (NAH-ree SHUK-tee VUN-dun uh-dhi-NEE-yum). 33 percent of Lok Sabha and state assembly seats for women. Lok Sabha passed it 454 to 2. Rajya Sabha, 214 to nothing. And yet it isn't operational.</Person1>
<Person2>Because the Act ties its commencement to two things that haven't happened yet — the first census after 2026, and the delimitation that follows. The earliest it can possibly apply is the 2029 general election. Which means a law passed with near-unanimous support across all parties is sitting in a legal waiting room because the predicate facts don't exist yet.</Person2>
<Person1>And that delimitation — redrawing constituency boundaries based on the 2027 census — is its own political minefield. Because state-wise Lok Sabha seats were frozen on the 1971 census.</Person1>
<Person2>Frozen by the 42nd Amendment in 1976, extended to "the first census after 2026" by the 84th Amendment in 2001. The bargain was, states that successfully brought down their fertility rates — mostly the South — wouldn't be punished by losing seats. Now that freeze is about to lift. UP and Bihar would gain. Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Karnataka would lose proportional influence. And tied up in the same knot is the 131st Amendment Bill from April 2026, which tried to enlarge the Lok Sabha and operationalise both delimitation and women's reservation in one package — and failed. 298 to 230, well short of the 352 it needed.</Person2>
<Person1>So three things are converging on the same window — delimitation in 2027, women's reservation possibly in 2029, and a failed amendment that was trying to handle both. The Constitution we have at this moment is genuinely in suspense about its own next chapter.</Person1>
<Person2>It is. And that's the thing to hold onto when people call the Constitution a settled document. 75 years in, the most consequential structural question — how many seats each state gets in the House of the People — is still open. The Constituent Assembly handed us a working machine. They did not hand us a finished one.</Person2>
<Person1>So when the 2027 census numbers come out, the real argument starts. And whatever Parliament does next will tell us what kind of federation we actually are.</Person1>
