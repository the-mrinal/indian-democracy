<Person1>Hi Aditya. Today I want to talk about the slip that arrives at your door two days before polling — the parchi (PUR-chee) — and the entire invisible machine that put it in your hand.</Person1>
<Person2>Hi Neha. That's the right place to start, because for sixteen chapters we've been talking about the Constitution, the ECI, the Lok Sabha (lohk SUB-hah) — the architecture. This chapter is the execution layer. The book we're leaning on is Shivam Shankar Singh's How to Win an Indian Election, written by someone who actually ran data and field operations for the BJP in Manipur in 2017 and Tripura in 2018. He names the line items. He names the numbers.</Person2>
<Person1>And the whole thing is built booth by booth. Not constituency by constituency — booth. One polling station, maybe 1,200 voters.</Person1>
<Person2>That's the smallest atom of Indian politics. And at every booth you have two people doing parallel work that most voters never notice. The Booth Level Officer, the BLO, is appointed by the Election Commission — usually a school teacher, an Anganwadi worker, a panchayat secretary. Their job is the electoral roll. The Booth Level Agent, the BLA, is appointed by the party. Their job is to shadow the BLO and make sure their party's voters get added and the opposition's questionable voters get challenged.</Person2>
<Person1>So the roll itself is contested. We talk about elections as if the voter list is just a neutral document the ECI hands out.</Person1>
<Person2>It's anything but neutral. Singh's blunt point is — the party that controls its BLA network controls the electoral roll at the most granular level. Adding one genuine supporter, deleting one shifted-away opposition voter — that's the cheapest vote-multiplier in Indian politics. Cheaper than rallies, cheaper than ads, cheaper than helicopter visits.</Person2>
<Person1>And then there's the panna pramukh (PUN-naa pra-MOOKH) model. This is the BJP innovation everyone talks about.</Person1>
<Person2>The math is almost embarrassingly simple. A double-sided page of the electoral roll has about 60 names on it. You assign one karyakarta — one cadre — to that page. They are personally responsible for those 60 voters. They verify them, they understand what each of those 60 households actually cares about, they deliver the parchi, and on poll day they make sure those 60 people walk to the booth.</Person2>
<Person1>So organisational strength stops being abstract. It becomes — do you have a named human being attached to every 60 voters in the country.</Person1>
<Person2>And once that exists, every other party has to build it. TMC has Banglar Garbo Mamata. The DMK has its volunteer cadre. YSRCP built one in Andhra. It's the model that won 2014 and was scaled to industrial size by 2019.</Person2>
<Person1>Let me push on something though. This sounds very clean — page chiefs, accountable coverage. In reality, how many of these panna pramukhs are actually doing the work versus existing on a spreadsheet?</Person1>
<Person2>Fair question. The honest answer is — wide variation. In a Gujarat or a UP booth where the party is dominant, the network is real. In a southern state where the BJP is weaker, a lot of those page chiefs are on paper. Singh's own framing acknowledges this — the panna pramukh model is an aspiration that the strongest parties approach and others fall short of.</Person2>
<Person1>Okay. So before you even get to the panna pramukh, the campaign has classified your booth. Friendly, hostile, or swing.</Person1>
<Person2>And that classification decides where every rupee goes. A friendly booth — somewhere the party historically wins by margin — gets get-out-the-vote treatment. Transport for the elderly, parchis, panna pramukhs working full tilt. A hostile booth gets the opposite — file objections to dummy voters, contest irregularities, just maintain a token presence. A swing booth — where the last few elections were decided by under 5 percent — gets saturation. Door-to-door, repeat visits, targeted welfare announcements.</Person2>
<Person1>So when a candidate visits your locality three times in a week, that's not affection. That's a swing-booth classification.</Person1>
<Person2>It's a spreadsheet decision. Which is — depending on your mood — either reassuring or deeply unromantic.</Person2>
<Person1>Before we leave the cadre layer — there's also the parchi itself, which is such a small thing but does so much work.</Person1>
<Person2>The parchi is a slip that the BLA hands to every voter on their page just before polling day. Booth number, your serial number on the voter list, the section you're in. Sounds trivial. But the friction it removes is enormous — you walk up, you quote the number, you're in. Parties without a real BLA network rely on the ECI's official slip distribution, which is more patchy, often late. So the same voter, in the same booth, has a measurably easier vote with a strong party network than without one.</Person2>
<Person1>Surveys are the other piece of the data layer, right? Before the campaign even starts.</Person1>
<Person2>Three layers. A baseline survey at T-minus-90 to T-minus-60 days that maps caste and community, key issues, candidate evaluation, anti-incumbency. Then tracking surveys every two to four weeks during the campaign, watching how things move. And on poll day itself, an internal exit poll booth by booth — that's what drives the last-minute GOTV calls in the final two hours. The big firms — CVoter, Axis My India, Today's Chanakya, Lokniti-CSDS — plus bespoke shops run by Prashant Kishor's I-PAC and Sunil Kanugolu.</Person2>
<Person1>Let's talk about the voter file itself. Because this is where the survey data meets the cadre network.</Person1>
<Person2>The voter roll is the spine, and then you merge things onto it. Mobile numbers, often collected at panchayat level. DBT beneficiary lists — PMAY for housing, Ujjwala (OOJ-wa-la) for LPG, PM-KISAN for farmers, Jan Dhan for bank accounts, Ladli Behna (LAAD-lee BEH-na) for women in MP and Maharashtra. Census tract data for caste and community by booth. A single voter file can carry dozens of attributes per person.</Person2>
<Person1>And then the same voter gets different WhatsApp messages depending on which attribute the party thinks will move them.</Person1>
<Person2>That's the model. The farmer gets a PM-KISAN message. The woman in the same household gets a Ladli Behna message. The young man gets something about jobs or a different issue entirely. Three messages, one house, all from the same campaign.</Person2>
<Person1>This is where Singh's argument about incumbency gets uncomfortable. Because he's basically saying — incumbency advantage in India isn't really about governance. It's about scheme branding.</Person1>
<Person2>That's his thesis. PMAY puts up roughly 4 crore housing units, each with a foundation-laying ceremony and a possession ceremony, both branded. Ujjwala — 10 crore LPG connections to women in BPL households. Jan Dhan — around 50 crore accounts, each one a recurring SMS opportunity every time a DBT lands. The scheme isn't just welfare. It's a permanent campaign asset.</Person2>
<Person1>And the challenger's counter is the victim card. My community has been historically excluded. Vote for me and I'll expand reservations, do a caste census, start a new cash transfer.</Person1>
<Person2>Both sides are running the same playbook from opposite ends. One says — look what I've delivered. The other says — look what you've been denied.</Person2>
<Person1>Let's talk about the IT cell. Because this is the part that's gone from a side operation in 2014 to the core of the campaign in 2024.</Person1>
<Person2>An IT cell today is five things in one. Content production — videos, memes, morning forwards. WhatsApp group management — thousands of small groups organised by booth, locality, profession, caste, language. Paid promotion on Facebook, Instagram, YouTube, X. Defensive monitoring of opposition narratives. And offensive operations — coordinated hashtag campaigns, targeting opposition leaders, what we politely call troll armies.</Person2>
<Person1>And in 2024 we got the deepfake election. AI-generated audio and video of major leaders saying things they never said.</Person1>
<Person2>The ECI updated the Model Code of Conduct and set up a fast-track grievance system. It was, by all honest accounts, only partially effective. By the time a deepfake is flagged and taken down, it has been forwarded across a few hundred WhatsApp groups. The genie doesn't go back in the bottle.</Person2>
<Person1>And before deepfakes, there was Cambridge Analytica. Which had a more specific India footprint than most people remember.</Person1>
<Person2>In 2018, when that scandal broke globally, the India angle was that Cambridge Analytica's local partner — Ovleno Business Intelligence, OBI — listed the BJP, the Congress, and the JD(U) all as clients. The MD of OBI was Amrish Tyagi, son of the senior JD(U) leader K.C. Tyagi. So it wasn't one party — it was the realisation that psychographic profiling and A/B-tested messaging had already crossed over from American campaigns to Indian ones.</Person2>
<Person1>Then there's the money. The official Lok Sabha candidate cap is 95 lakh. What does a competitive seat actually cost?</Person1>
<Person2>Singh's breakdown puts actual spending at several crores. The line items are revealing. Cash to booth-level workers and panna pramukhs — 20 to 30 percent. Vehicles, fuel, drivers — 10 to 15. Rallies, sound, staging — another 10 to 15. Surveys and polling — only 2 to 5. And then a line item called envelope politics — cash, liquor, gifts on poll-eve — 10 to 20 percent. The official return that the candidate files with the ECI bears almost no relationship to any of this.</Person2>
<Person1>And then poll day itself is choreographed by the hour.</Person1>
<Person2>By the hour. 6 am — every booth karyakarta is activated, personal calls to the most committed voters. 7 to noon is the high-yield window — get your friendly voters in early, before the queue gets long, before the day gets hot. Noon to 4 is the lull, that's when you chase the persuadable swing voters. 4 to 6 is the final push — panna pramukhs literally cross off their pages, see who hasn't shown up, send transport. After poll close, EVMs sealed in presence of polling agents, transported under armed escort to strongrooms.</Person2>
<Person1>And then the campaign doesn't even end on poll day. There's a post-result phase.</Person1>
<Person2>Thank-you tours, which look ceremonial but are actually the start of the next election. Rewards for loyal cadres — paid roles in MP and MLA offices, party positions, contracts, government appointments. And quiet punishments for the booths that underperformed — your panna pramukh next time gets the harder section, or none at all. The implicit deal is — you deliver votes, we deliver patronage to the people who delivered the votes. That's how the booth network stays alive between elections.</Person2>
<Person1>So if you put Laxmikanth and Singh on the same desk — one tells you the rules, the other tells you the game inside the rules.</Person1>
<Person2>And neither is complete without the other. The Constitution says MPs are elected by FPTP. Singh adds — yes, on the back of booth-by-booth panna pramukh networks. The MCC restricts campaign behaviour. Singh adds — and yet envelope politics persists. The cap is 95 lakh. Actual spending is 5 to 50 times that. The ECI's BLO maintains the roll. The parties' BLAs shape it.</Person2>
<Person1>The question that keeps coming back to me is — if the panna pramukh model, the data merges, the IT cell — if all of this is now the cost of entry to compete seriously, what does that do to a brand new party? Or to a candidate without the infrastructure of a national machine behind them?</Person1>
<Person2>It raises the floor. The Aam Aadmi Party in 2013, in one city, with a single issue, could break through. To do that today, across a state, against a fully built panna pramukh network with merged voter files and a 24-hour IT cell — the entry ticket is an order of magnitude higher than it was a decade ago. Which means the next disruption probably won't look like AAP. It'll look like whoever figures out the layer that comes after this one.</Person2>
