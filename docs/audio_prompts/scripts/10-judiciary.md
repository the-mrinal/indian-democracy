<Person1>Hi Aditya. Judiciary day. <Person2>Hi Neha. Those numbers are the punchline, but let's start at the top of the pyramid because the shape itself is unusual. India has a single integrated judiciary — one ladder, not two. In the United States, federal courts and state courts run in parallel, two separate systems. We collapsed that into one. A property dispute in a Madurai munsif court and a Centre-State row at the Supreme Court are technically on the same staircase.</Person2>
<Person1>One staircase. Walk me up it.</Person1>
<Person2>At the base, subordinate courts in every district — civil on one side, criminal on the other. Above them, the District and Sessions Judge, often the same person wearing two hats. Above that, the 25 High Courts. And at the apex, on Tilak Marg in Delhi, the Supreme Court — the Chief Justice plus up to 33 other judges, a strength last raised in 2019. Appeals flow upward. That's the whole architecture.</Person2>
<Person1>25 High Courts feels like a lot, but it's actually fewer than the number of states. Some courts cover multiple states?</Person1>
<Person2>Five of them do. Bombay High Court covers Maharashtra, Goa, plus Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu. Gauhati covers four north-eastern states. Punjab and Haryana share one. Jammu and Kashmir share a High Court with Ladakh. And the sizes are wildly uneven — Allahabad High Court is sanctioned for about 160 judges, Sikkim High Court has three. Three judges for the whole state.</Person2>
<Person1>That's the inequality nobody talks about. Now, the Supreme Court. The current Chief Justice is Surya Kant (SOOR-yah kunt), sworn in last November. He's the 53rd CJI.</Person1>
<Person2>And the 50th was D.Y. Chandrachud (chun-druh-CHOOD), who only retired in November 2024. Then Sanjiv Khanna for six months. Then B.R. Gavai for six months. Then Surya Kant. Four Chief Justices in about a year. That's a function of the seniority-based appointment system — short tenures at the top are baked in, which is its own problem for institutional continuity.</Person2>
<Person1>Let's talk about how those judges get there in the first place. Because the appointment process is genuinely strange — it's not in the Constitution. It's a system the judges invented for themselves.</Person1>
<Person2>The Collegium. And the way it came about is one of the great institutional power-grabs in Indian history. Article 124 says the President appoints judges in consultation with the CJI and others. Three cases — 1981, 1993, 1998 — gradually flipped "consultation" into "concurrence" into "the CJI plus the four senior-most judges decide, and the government has to sign off." The First Judges Case gave primacy to the executive. The Second Judges Case took it back. The Third Judges Case expanded the panel to five.</Person2>
<Person1>So the Supreme Court read the Constitution and decided the Constitution meant the opposite of what it said.</Person1>
<Person2>That's the uncharitable version, and it's not entirely wrong. The charitable version is that after the Emergency — after Indira Gandhi superseded three judges to install A.N. Ray as Chief Justice in 1973 — the Court decided executive control over appointments was a structural threat to independence. The Collegium was the over-correction.</Person2>
<Person1>And then in 2014, Parliament tried to take it back.</Person1>
<Person2>The NJAC. The 99th Constitutional Amendment, passed unanimously by Parliament, ratified by 16 state legislatures. It would have created a National Judicial Appointments Commission — three judges, the Law Minister, and two eminent persons chosen by the PM, CJI, and Leader of the Opposition. In 2015, a five-judge bench struck it down four to one. They said judicial primacy in appointments is part of the Basic Structure, and adding a Law Minister to the room compromises independence.</Person2>
<Person1>One Parliament, sixteen state legislatures, struck down by four judges. That's the entire debate about judicial overreach in one sentence.</Person1>
<Person2>It is. And the dissent — Justice Chelameswar — wrote that the Collegium is opaque, that no minutes are kept, that brothers and sons of judges get appointed at suspicious rates. He wasn't wrong about any of that. But the majority felt the cure was worse than the disease. So we're stuck with the Collegium, much criticised, legally entrenched, and currently sitting on about 30 percent vacancies in the High Courts.</Person2>
<Person1>Let's switch from how the Court is built to what it actually does. The jurisdiction list is long — original, appellate, writ, advisory, review, curative. For someone who's never been near a courtroom, what's the one that matters most?</Person1>
<Person2>Article 32 and Article 226. The writ jurisdictions. Article 32 lets you go directly to the Supreme Court to enforce a Fundamental Right — Ambedkar (AHM-bed-kar) called it the heart and soul of the Constitution. Article 226 is the High Court version, and it's actually wider, because it covers any legal right, not just Fundamental Rights. That's why most writ petitions in India are filed in High Courts. The Supreme Court is technically the apex, but the High Courts do the bulk of the rights work.</Person2>
<Person1>And then there's the way most cases actually reach the Supreme Court — the SLP. Special Leave Petition. Article 136.</Person1>
<Person2>Discretionary. The Court can take up an appeal from any judgment, any tribunal, any High Court, for any reason it finds sufficient. It was meant to be exceptional. It became the main gateway. Which is part of why there are 80,000 pending cases — the Court is its own bottleneck, agreeing to hear far more than its capacity.</Person2>
<Person1>I want to talk about PIL. Public Interest Litigation. Because this is the one most people have actually encountered, even if they don't realise it. The Vishaka guidelines on sexual harassment came from a PIL. So did a lot of the Ganga cleanup orders. The midday meal scheme directions. How did the Court give itself this power?</Person1>
<Person2>By relaxing what's called locus standi — the rule that only the person directly harmed can approach the court. In the late 1970s and early 80s, two judges in particular — Krishna Iyer and P.N. Bhagwati — said any member of the public with sufficient interest could write to the Court on behalf of bonded labourers, prisoners, the environment, anyone who couldn't reach the court themselves. Postcards from journalists became writ petitions. That was the revolution.</Person2>
<Person1>And the cost of that revolution?</Person1>
<Person2>Judicial overreach is the polite phrase. The Court started running prisons, supervising forests, monitoring pollution levels, telling the executive how to allocate funds. Critics — and not just political ones — argue this is bad for democracy because unelected judges are making policy. Defenders say nobody else was doing it. Both are right, which is why the debate never ends.</Person2>
<Person1>Before we get to removal, there's a whole parallel system the chapter mentions — tribunals. CAT, NCLT, NGT, ITAT. Most people interact with these long before they interact with a High Court. Where do they sit in the pyramid?</Person1>
<Person2>They were grafted onto the Constitution by the 42nd Amendment in 1976 — Articles 323A and 323B. The idea was specialised, expert bodies handling service disputes, tax, environment, company law — taking pressure off the regular courts. The 42nd Amendment also tried to make their decisions final, ousting High Court jurisdiction. That part lasted about 20 years.</Person2>
<Person1>And the case that undid it?</Person1>
<Person2>L. Chandra Kumar, 1997. A Constitution Bench held that judicial review by the High Courts under Article 226 and the Supreme Court under Article 32 is itself part of the Basic Structure. Parliament cannot oust it. So every tribunal order, however specialised, is still subject to writ scrutiny by a High Court. Which is good for rights, less good for the pendency problem we started with — every tribunal decision now has a built-in appellate detour.</Person2>
<Person1>Speaking of unelected, let's talk about removal. Because the one number from this chapter that genuinely surprised me — zero. No Supreme Court judge has ever been removed. Ever.</Person1>
<Person2>Not for want of trying. The closest call was Justice V. Ramaswami in 1993. The Inquiry Committee found him guilty on 11 of 14 charges — including spending public money on carpets and security at his official residence when he was Chief Justice of Punjab and Haryana. The Lok Sabha vote: 196 for removal, zero against. And 205 Congress MPs abstained. The motion failed because removal needs a majority of the total membership of the House, not just those voting. A whipped abstention killed it.</Person2>
<Person1>So the bar is technically high, but the real bar is political. You need 100 Lok Sabha members or 50 Rajya Sabha members to sign the motion, then a special majority in both Houses in the same session. And if any one party decides to walk out, you're done.</Person1>
<Person2>That's the structural problem with impeachment as a remedy. It's so high a bar that in 75 years, nobody has cleared it. Which means in practice, the only check on a sitting judge is the judge's own conscience and the Collegium's willingness to not elevate someone problematic. That's a thin reed.</Person2>
<Person1>Let me ask about one more tool, because it's been in the news a lot. Article 142. "Complete justice." The Court used it in 2025 to deem 10 Tamil Nadu bills to have received the Governor's assent. That sounds enormous.</Person1>
<Person2>It is. Article 142 is a one-line provision that says the Supreme Court can pass any order necessary for doing complete justice in a matter before it. It's been used in the Bhopal Gas settlement. It was used in 2019 to direct the Ayodhya land allotment. And now it's being used to substitute for a Governor's signature. The text is open-ended; the use of it has become more ambitious. That's the running tension in the Court today.</Person2>
<Person1>And the third pressure point you mentioned at the start — Centre-State frictions. The Court is increasingly the referee in fights between opposition-ruled states and the Union.</Person1>
<Person2>Governor disputes, ED and CBI investigations, Article 356 threats, the Article 370 abrogation challenge, electoral bonds.
