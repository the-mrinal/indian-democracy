<Person1>Hi Aditya. Two years of Indian politics in one episode — 2024 Lok Sabha (lohk SUB-hah) to the May 2026 state results. And I want to start with the one line that still doesn't feel real to me: Suvendu Adhikari is the Chief Minister of West Bengal.</Person1>
<Person2>Hi Neha. Yeah. That sentence would have got you laughed out of any newsroom even eighteen months ago. A BJP Chief Minister in Bengal — the state where the party didn't even exist as a serious force until about 2019. We'll get there. But the runway to that result actually starts on June 4th, 2024, the day the BJP discovered it couldn't form a government on its own steam.</Person2>
<Person1>Right. Counting day. I remember refreshing the ECI portal at lunch and the BJP number just stopped climbing somewhere around 240. After 303 in 2019, 240 felt like a different planet.</Person1>
<Person2>240, with 272 as the majority mark — short by 32. The NDA as a whole landed at roughly 293, the INDIA bloc at 234. So for the first time since 2014, Modi had to actually count Chandrababu Naidu's TDP and Nitish Kumar's JD(U) into his government. Sixteen seats and twelve seats — suddenly load-bearing. And Rahul Gandhi as Leader of the Opposition for the first formally recognised LOP since 2014 — which is not a ceremonial title. The LOP sits on the selection committees that pick the Lokpal (LOHK-paal), the CVC, the Chief Information Commissioner, and after the 2023 Act, the Chief Election Commissioner.</Person2>
<Person1>And on the same day, two firsts buried under the bigger story. Om Birla re-elected Speaker — only the second person ever after Balram Jakhar to serve consecutive terms in that chair. And BJP opened its account in Kerala for the first time, with Suresh Gopi winning Thrissur.</Person1>
<Person2>The Speaker decides what's a Money Bill, which Bills get sent to committee, who gets disqualified under anti-defection — having the same person hold that chair across two Parliaments matters more than it sounds. But the place where the BJP really cracked open in 2024 was Uttar Pradesh. 80 seats, the biggest single prize, and the Samajwadi Party ended up with 37 and the BJP with 33. The Faizabad seat — the constituency that contains Ayodhya — went to the SP, months after the Ram temple consecration. People read a lot into that. My more boring reading is that even in a temple-town constituency, voters were also thinking about contractor wages, paper leaks, and the price of dal.</Person2>
<Person1>The southern picture in 2024 is also worth a beat. Andhra Pradesh held its Assembly and Lok Sabha together, TDP-BJP-JSP swept, YSRCP collapsed, Chandrababu Naidu sworn in as CM. Tamil Nadu the DMK-led INDIA alliance took 22 of 39 by themselves with allies splitting the rest. Even in 2024, the south was already telling a different story than the Hindi belt. And then there's Maharashtra — the Mahayuti, with Shinde's Shiv Sena and Ajit Pawar's NCP, the engineered splits, won only 17 of 48. The MVA took 31. Five months later, in the November 2024 Assembly election, the same Mahayuti won 235 of 288 seats. Same voters, same parties, opposite verdict. How do you even narrate that?</Person1>
<Person2>You don't, easily. That's why "the Modi wave" as a thesis is basically over. The chapter puts it well — regional context, candidate selection, scheme delivery, ground execution. The Ladki Bahin cash transfer scheme launched between those two elections is the single biggest variable people point to. But the larger point is, the same electorate can deliver a Lok Sabha verdict and an Assembly verdict that contradict each other, and we have to stop being surprised by it. Maharashtra also produced one detail worth pausing on — no party crossed the ten-percent threshold for the Leader of Opposition post. For the first time in over six decades.</Person2>
<Person1>So at the state level, in one of India's most politically literate states, there is no formally recognised opposition leader. The same threshold that kept the LOP post empty at the Centre from 2014 to 2024 — now operating in reverse in Maharashtra.</Person1>
<Person2>Symmetric rule, same hollowing-out, different state. And while we're on the November 2024 calendar — Jharkhand went the other way. JMM-led INDIA bloc took 56 of 81, NDA stuck at 24, Hemant Soren back as CM after his arrest and release earlier that year. So in the same month, Maharashtra hands the NDA a landslide and Jharkhand hands it a drubbing. That's the pattern, repeating. Walk us through the rest of the cycle, because a lot happened in a hurry.</Person2>
<Person1>Haryana in October 2024 — exit polls had Congress sweeping, and then BJP won 48 of 90. Nayab Singh Saini sworn in for a full term. Then J&K had its first Assembly election since Article 370 was abrogated — National Conference 42, BJP 29, and Omar Abdullah became Chief Minister of what's now a Union Territory.</Person1>
<Person2>An elected CM with a Lieutenant Governor sitting above him. Constitutionally awkward, and we'll be litigating that arrangement for years. The Haryana exit-poll miss was the first warning that the post-2024 read of "the BJP is in trouble" was being over-extrapolated. Then February 2025 — Delhi. BJP wins 48 of 70, AAP down to 22, Congress zero. First BJP government in Delhi since Sushma Swaraj in 1998. Twenty-seven years in the wilderness in their own national capital — and the AAP model, the schools, the mohalla clinics, the free electricity narrative that everyone studied as a template, voters in Delhi looked at it and said, we want something else now.</Person2>
<Person1>Bihar in November 2025 was the one I found hardest to read. NDA 202 out of 243. Mahagathbandhan 35. That's not an election, that's a flattening.</Person1>
<Person2>And then the post-script — Nitish Kumar sworn in for a tenth term, resigns in April 2026 to go to the Rajya Sabha, and Samrat Chaudhary becomes Bihar's first BJP Chief Minister. If you'd told my history-teacher self in 2005 that one day Bihar would have a BJP CM, I would have asked what you were smoking. The state of Lalu, of the Mandal moment — a BJP CM. The map of where the BJP can and cannot govern has just been redrawn at the corners.</Person2>
<Person1>Which brings us to the headline. West Bengal, May 4th, 2026. BJP 45 percent vote share, majority of 294 seats. Mamata loses Bhabanipur to Suvendu Adhikari — the same Suvendu who beat her in Nandigram in 2021. Won from both seats this time. Fifteen years of TMC rule, ended.</Person1>
<Person2>And the turnout figure is the one nobody can quite metabolise — about 93 percent. The highest turnout in any Indian state election ever recorded. On a roll that was 11 percent smaller than 2024, after the Special Intensive Revision deleted about nine million names. Which brings us to the SIR controversy, and this is where the chapter gets uncomfortable, and rightly so.</Person2>
<Person1>Walk through that, because I think a lot of people only half-followed it.</Person1>
<Person2>Between October 2025 and March 2026 the ECI ran the SIR in Bengal. Nine million entries deleted — roughly twelve percent of the existing roll. TMC said genuine voters were being struck off, especially in Muslim-majority constituencies. BJP said bogus entries and illegal migrants were being cleaned out. Petitions in Calcutta High Court, in the Supreme Court, all pending. The election went ahead on the revised roll.</Person2>
<Person1>So the question of whether that result is the verdict of the people of Bengal, or the verdict of the people of Bengal minus nine million of them, is going to be argued for the next decade.</Person1>
<Person2>And the answer matters less to the BJP than it does to the legitimacy of the ECI as an institution. Because if the SIR set a precedent for what an electoral roll revision can do in a politically contested state, every future state cycle is going to have a fight about the rolls before there's even a fight about the candidates. That's a new front.</Person2>
<Person1>Tamil Nadu on the same day was the other one I didn't see coming. Vijay's party — TVK — 108 seats. M.K. Stalin loses his own seat in Kolathur. The DMK-AIADMK monopoly since 1967 — gone.</Person1>
<Person2>Fifty-nine years of those two parties trading power, broken by a film actor whose party was less than two and a half years old on polling day. Pre-poll and exit polls under-estimated TVK by about seventy-one seats on average — which is less a polling failure and more an admission that there's no framework yet for measuring what a Vijay vote means in a constituency. Stalin resigned the next morning. Kerala broke the other way — UDF 102 of 140, Pinarayi Vijayan retained his own seat but resigned anyway, thirteen of twenty-one LDF ministers lost.</Person2>
<Person1>The state that re-elected the Left in 2021 — kicked them out in 2026. So if you zoom out across the southern states on May 4th, Tamil Nadu went to TVK, Kerala went to UDF, Telangana is still Congress, Karnataka is still Congress. The south voted in five different directions, but not one of them was the BJP.</Person1>
<Person2>The chapter's phrase for this is "structurally outside BJP-NDA dominance," and I think that's right. And that southern resistance is going to collide with delimitation. The next census is scheduled for March 2027, the constituency map gets redrawn after that, and the south loses seats while the north gains them. The failed 131st Amendment in April 2026 was the opening skirmish — if you can't even get the preliminary amendment through, the actual delimitation is going to be the biggest constitutional fight of the next Lok Sabha. Layer on Women's Reservation, which only kicks in after delimitation, and you've got two explosive pieces of representation politics tied together in one knot.</Person2>
<Person1>So here's the picture after the cycle. BJP or NDA governs the Hindi belt, most of the west, Bengal for the first time, all of the north-east, and Delhi. Opposition holds the south plus Punjab, Himachal, Jharkhand, J&K. Coalition-dependent Lok Sabha. Recognised LOP. Sharper Centre-state friction with at least eight major opposition-ruled governments.</Person1>
<Person2>And a Prime Minister in his third consecutive term — only the second person after Nehru (NEH-roo) to manage that — running a coalition government for the first time in his career. Modi the negotiator is a Modi we haven't really watched before. Bill timetabling slows, the ordinance route gets harder, committee referrals go up. The mechanics of Parliament start mattering again because the arithmetic isn't pre-decided.</Person2>
<Person1>The 2024-to-2026 stretch basically dismantled two ideas at once. That the BJP couldn't lose its majority, and that the BJP couldn't win in Bengal. Both turned out to be wrong inside of two years.</Person1>
<Person2>Which leaves the only question worth asking now — what's the next thing about Indian electoral politics that we're all sure is true, that the 2029 cycle is going to dismantle?</Person2>
