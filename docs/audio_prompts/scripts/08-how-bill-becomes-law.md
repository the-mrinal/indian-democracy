<Person1>Hi Aditya. I want to do the pipeline today — how a bill actually becomes law. Because every time a headline says "Parliament passes Bill X," there's about six months of plumbing nobody talks about.</Person1>
<Person2>Hi Neha. Six months on the fast track. Some take years. A few die in committee, and roughly one in a hundred dies at the President's desk. So when you read "Parliament passed it," what you're really reading is the last paragraph of a very long memo. Where do you want to start — the four kinds of bills, or the journey itself?</Person2>
<Person1>Start with the four kinds. Because I think most people, me included until recently, just imagine "a bill" as one thing.</Person1>
<Person2>It's not. The Constitution distinguishes four. Ordinary bills — anything not in the other three buckets. Money bills, defined strictly by Article 110 — only fiscal matters. Financial bills, which are a hybrid. And constitutional amendment bills under Article 368. The distinction matters because each one travels a different route, with different majorities, and different powers for the Rajya Sabha (RAH-juh SUB-hah).</Person2>
<Person1>Let's walk the ordinary bill path first, because that's the default. The chapter says three readings. What's actually happening in each?</Person1>
<Person2>The first reading is almost ceremonial. The minister stands up, asks for leave, the title and objects are read, the bill is published in the Gazette. No debate. That's it. If you were watching live you'd think nothing happened.</Person2>
<Person1>So the real work starts after that.</Person1>
<Person2>After that, most government bills get referred to a Departmentally Related Standing Committee. There are 24 of them, each tied to a ministry or group of ministries. The committee can call public submissions, hear experts, sit on the thing for weeks. This is where the substantive negotiation happens — clauses get rewritten, dangerous provisions sometimes get pulled out. For really big bills they'll set up a Joint Parliamentary Committee or JPC — Bofors in '87, 2G in 2011, the Data Protection Bill in 2022.</Person2>
<Person1>And then back to the floor for the second reading?</Person1>
<Person2>Right. The second reading has two halves. First a general debate on the principles, then clause-by-clause voting with amendments. The third reading is short — a final debate on the bill as a whole, motion is "That the bill be passed," simple majority of those present and voting. That's the originating House done. Then the whole thing repeats in the other House.</Person2>
<Person1>Here's where I want to push you, Aditya. You said committees do the real work. But the chapter has a number that bothers me — committee referral rate was around 70 percent in the 14th and 15th Lok Sabha. In the 17th Lok Sabha it dropped to about 25 percent.</Person1>
<Person2>It bothers me too. That's the unsung backbone getting quietly removed. When three out of four bills skip the committee stage, what you're really saying is: the floor will vote on whatever the executive hands over, with no expert scrutiny, no public submissions, no rewritten clauses. Parliament starts to look less like a deliberative body and more like a stamping office.</Person2>
<Person1>Which connects to something I keep wondering. When the news says "the bill was passed in 12 minutes" — I remember this from the farm laws — that's the third reading we're seeing, not the whole journey?</Person1>
<Person2>Often it's the second and third together, on a single afternoon, with no committee in between. The 12-minute headline is technically the law working as designed. But it's the design being used at its thinnest setting. Which is why people on both sides of the aisle get nervous about it.</Person2>
<Person1>Okay, what happens when the two Houses don't agree? Lok Sabha passes something, Rajya Sabha rejects it, or sits on it.</Person1>
<Person2>That's a deadlock. Three triggers — outright rejection, amendments the originating House won't accept, or the other House just not acting for six months. When that happens, Article 108 lets the President summon both Houses to a joint sitting. Combined strength, simple majority. And because the Lok Sabha has 543 members and the Rajya Sabha has 245, the lower House's view effectively wins.</Person2>
<Person1>How often has that actually happened?</Person1>
<Person2>Three times. Ever. Dowry Prohibition Bill in 1961, Banking Service Commission Repeal in '78, and POTA — the Prevention of Terrorism Act — in 2002. That's it. Seventy-five years of Parliament, three joint sittings. Most deadlocks get resolved by quiet negotiation in the corridors long before anyone has to schedule a Central Hall.</Person2>
<Person1>And Article 108 has carve-outs.</Person1>
<Person2>Two big ones. No joint sitting for money bills — the Lok Sabha's word is already final there. And no joint sitting for constitutional amendments — each House has to clear those on its own steam, by special majority.</Person2>
<Person1>Let's go to money bills, because this is the one I find genuinely tricky. The Aadhaar Act was passed as a money bill. People still argue about whether that was legitimate.</Person1>
<Person2>And the argument matters, because the money bill route is essentially a Rajya Sabha bypass. Article 110 lists exactly what counts — taxes, borrowing, Consolidated Fund, that family of things. Introduced only in the Lok Sabha, on the President's prior recommendation. Sent to the Rajya Sabha, which can only recommend amendments, has 14 days to return it, and if it sits on the thing past 14 days, the bill is deemed passed.</Person2>
<Person1>So the upper House is reduced to a 14-day suggestion box.</Person1>
<Person2>By design, for genuinely fiscal matters. The controversy is who decides what counts as fiscal. The Speaker certifies — and under Article 110(3), that certification is final. Aadhaar was certified a money bill in 2016. The Puttaswamy bench upheld it in 2018, with Justice Chandrachud dissenting that the certification was wrong. Then in 2019, the Rojer Mathew bench referred the whole question of judicial review of the Speaker's certification to a larger bench. Still pending. So we have an essential federal mechanism and a contested loophole sitting in the same provision.</Person2>
<Person1>Constitutional amendments next. This is the heaviest route — special majority in each House, sometimes ratification by half the states. What's the logic of the three different thresholds?</Person1>
<Person2>The logic is: the more federal the stake, the more states you need on board. Simple majority for the lightest stuff. Special majority — total membership of each House, plus two-thirds of those present — for most amendments, including fundamental rights. And special majority plus ratification by half the state legislatures when you're touching anything that affects the Centre-state balance — the President's election, the Supreme Court, the Seventh Schedule, Article 368 itself.</Person2>
<Person1>And the President can't refuse assent to a constitutional amendment.</Person1>
<Person2>Can't return it, can't sit on it. The 24th Amendment in 1971 specifically nailed that door shut, after a period when Indira Gandhi (IN-dee-rah GAHN-dee) and the Supreme Court were essentially fighting over how much Parliament could amend. Two years later you get Kesavananda Bharati and the basic structure doctrine — Parliament can amend, but it cannot destroy the Constitution's basic structure. So the constraint moved from the President's desk to the courtroom.</Person2>
<Person1>Speaking of the President's desk — Article 111, four options. Assent, withhold, return, or just sit on it. That last one is wild. There's no time limit?</Person1>
<Person2>No time limit in the text. Which is how President Zail Singh (zail singh) pocket-vetoed the Indian Post Office Amendment Bill in 1986. He didn't sign it, didn't return it, didn't reject it. The bill simply sat. That was a Rajiv Gandhi (RAH-jeev GAHN-dee) government bill, a President from the same party, and yet — nothing moved.</Person2>
<Person1>It's almost embarrassing as a design feature. A whole branch of government can be stopped by silence.</Person1>
<Person2>It's not embarrassing if you assume good faith. The drafters assumed the President would act in reasonable time because that's what constitutional officers do. The assumption held, mostly. Where it stops holding is at the state level — the Tamil Nadu Governor sat on 10 bills for over a year, and last April the Supreme Court used Article 142 to deem them assented to. That's a remarkable move — the court essentially saying, if you won't act, we'll act for you.</Person2>
<Person1>And ordinances. Walk me through that, because every government uses them and they always feel slightly suspect.</Person1>
<Person2>Article 123. Parliament's not in session, the President is satisfied that immediate action is needed, an ordinance gets promulgated. It has the same force as a law. But — it must be laid before both Houses when they reassemble, and it dies six weeks after that unless Parliament approves it. The temptation, of course, is to re-promulgate the moment it's about to lapse, dodging Parliament indefinitely. The Supreme Court called that out in Krishna Kumar Singh in 2017 — repeated re-promulgation is a fraud on the Constitution.</Person2>
<Person1>Before we close, the procedural devices on the floor. Question Hour, Zero Hour, calling attention, cut motions. These feel like the texture of a real Parliament.</Person1>
<Person2>They are the texture. Question Hour from 11 to noon — starred questions get oral answers and supplementaries, unstarred get written replies. Then Zero Hour, an Indian innovation from 1962, completely unscripted. Calling attention motions, adjournment motions, the famous no-confidence motion that needs 50 MPs to admit. Cut motions on demands for grants — the token cut of 100 rupees just to register disapproval. None of these pass a law. All of them make a Parliament a Parliament rather than an auditorium.</Person2>
<Person1>The pipeline is precise. What worries me is that the pipeline is only as good as the parts you actually use. Skip the committee, certify as money bill, ordinance the controversial stuff, pocket-veto the inconvenient — the same constitution that gives you deliberation also gives you every shortcut around it.</Person1>
<Person2>And the next decade will tell us which version we're choosing. Whether the committee referral rate climbs back toward 70 percent. Whether the money bill question gets a clean answer from a larger bench. Whether governors and presidents accept that silence is no longer a constitutional option. The pipeline exists. The question is whether we still want to walk through it.</Person2>
