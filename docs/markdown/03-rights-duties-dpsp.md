# Rights, Duties & Directive Principles

<p class="lede">Three lists sit at the heart of the Constitution: <strong>Fundamental Rights</strong> (Part III) that the State cannot violate; <strong>Directive Principles</strong> (Part IV) that the State must strive towards; and <strong>Fundamental Duties</strong> (Part IVA) expected of every citizen. The first you can enforce in court. The second you cannot. The third has no penalty. Together, they define what it means to be a citizen of India.</p>

## 1 · Fundamental Rights (Part III, Articles 12–35)

Fundamental Rights are guarantees against the State, enforceable directly in the Supreme Court under **Article 32** and in High Courts under **Article 226**. Ambedkar called Article 32 the **"heart and soul of the Constitution"** — without it, the rights would be paper. The six categories ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_rights_in_India)):

### 1.1 Right to Equality (Articles 14–18)

| Article | Right |
|---|---|
| 14 | Equality before the law and equal protection of the laws |
| 15 | Prohibition of discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth |
| 16 | Equality of opportunity in public employment |
| 17 | Abolition of "untouchability" |
| 18 | Abolition of titles (military and academic distinctions excluded) |

Article 15(4)–(6) and 16(4)–(6) enable affirmative action — reservations for SCs/STs/OBCs and, since the 103rd Amendment (2019), for Economically Weaker Sections.

### 1.2 Right to Freedom (Articles 19–22)

**Article 19(1)** guarantees six freedoms to citizens:

- (a) freedom of speech and expression
- (b) freedom to assemble peaceably and without arms
- (c) freedom to form associations or unions
- (d) freedom to move freely throughout India
- (e) freedom to reside and settle in any part of India
- (g) freedom to practise any profession, or to carry on any occupation, trade or business

(Article 19(1)(f) — the right to property — was removed by the 44th Amendment, 1978.)

Each freedom is subject to "reasonable restrictions" enumerated in Articles 19(2)–(6) — sovereignty, integrity, public order, decency, morality, contempt of court, defamation, incitement to offence, etc.

- **Article 20** — Protection in respect of conviction for offences: no *ex post facto* law, no double jeopardy, no self-incrimination.
- **Article 21** — **Right to life and personal liberty**: "No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law." The Supreme Court has read into this an expansive bundle of derivative rights — privacy (*K.S. Puttaswamy* 2017), dignity, livelihood, education, shelter, health, clean environment, speedy trial.
- **Article 21A** (inserted by the 86th Amendment, 2002) — Free and compulsory education for children aged 6–14.
- **Article 22** — Protection against arrest and detention; but the protections do not apply to enemy aliens or those held under preventive detention laws.

### 1.3 Right against Exploitation (Articles 23–24)

- **Article 23** — Prohibition of human trafficking and forced labour (*begar*).
- **Article 24** — Prohibition of employment of children below 14 in factories, mines, or other hazardous work.

### 1.4 Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25–28)

- **Article 25** — Freedom of conscience and free profession, practice and propagation of religion.
- **Article 26** — Freedom to manage religious affairs.
- **Article 27** — No person can be compelled to pay any tax specifically for the promotion of any religion.
- **Article 28** — Freedom from religious instruction in wholly state-funded educational institutions.

### 1.5 Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29–30)

- **Article 29** — Protection of language, script and culture of minorities.
- **Article 30** — Right of religious and linguistic minorities to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice (without discrimination by the State in granting aid).

### 1.6 Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32)

The right to **move the Supreme Court directly** for the enforcement of any Fundamental Right. The Court may issue five writs (borrowed from English common law):

| Writ | Used for |
|---|---|
| **Habeas Corpus** | "Produce the body" — challenges illegal detention |
| **Mandamus** | "We command" — directs a public authority to perform its duty |
| **Prohibition** | Stops a lower court / tribunal from exceeding its jurisdiction |
| **Certiorari** | Quashes an order of a lower court / tribunal made in excess of jurisdiction |
| **Quo Warranto** | "By what authority?" — challenges a person's right to hold a public office |

High Courts under Article 226 can issue the same writs **for the enforcement of any legal right** — not just fundamental rights. Article 226 is therefore **wider** than Article 32 in scope.

<div class="callout warn">
  <strong>Two important deletions and one addition</strong>
  <strong>Removed:</strong> Right to Property (Art. 19(1)(f) and Art. 31) — by the 44th Amendment (1978). Property is now only a constitutional/legal right under <strong>Article 300A</strong>: "No person shall be deprived of his property save by authority of law." You cannot directly approach the Supreme Court under Art. 32 for a property dispute.<br>
  <strong>Added:</strong> Right to Education (Art. 21A) — by the 86th Amendment (2002), operationalised by the RTE Act, 2009.
</div>

## 2 · Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV, Articles 36–51)

Borrowed from the Irish Constitution. The Directive Principles are **non-justiciable** — Article 37 declares them "fundamental in the governance of the country" but "not enforceable by any court." Their purpose: to direct the State towards a welfare-oriented social order. Classified into three doctrinal groups ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directive_Principles_of_State_Policy)):

### Socialist principles
- Art. 38 — promote welfare; minimise inequalities
- Art. 39 — adequate livelihood, equal pay, no concentration of wealth, protection of children
- Art. 39A — equal justice and free legal aid
- Art. 41 — right to work, education, public assistance
- Art. 42 — just/humane conditions of work, maternity relief
- Art. 43 — living wage, decent standard of life
- Art. 43A — worker participation in management

### Gandhian principles
- Art. 40 — organisation of village panchayats
- Art. 43B — promotion of cooperative societies
- Art. 46 — promotion of educational and economic interests of SC/ST and other weaker sections
- Art. 47 — prohibition of intoxicating drinks; improvement of public health
- Art. 48 — prohibition of cow slaughter; organisation of agriculture on modern lines

### Liberal-Intellectual principles
- Art. 44 — **Uniform Civil Code** for all citizens
- Art. 45 — early childhood care/education for children under 6 (post 86th Amdt)
- Art. 48A — protection of environment, forests and wildlife
- Art. 49 — protection of monuments of national importance
- Art. 50 — separation of judiciary from executive
- Art. 51 — promotion of international peace and security

### Conflict with Fundamental Rights — *Minerva Mills* resolution

For decades, courts wrestled with whether DPSPs could override Fundamental Rights. The 42nd Amendment had inserted Article 31C, giving DPSPs broad primacy. In **Minerva Mills (1980)**, the Supreme Court held that **the balance between Part III (Rights) and Part IV (DPSPs) is itself part of the basic structure** — neither side can be allowed to extinguish the other. The Indian model is thus a careful tension, not a hierarchy.

## 3 · Fundamental Duties (Part IVA, Article 51A)

Added by the **42nd Amendment, 1976**, on the recommendation of the **Swaran Singh Committee**. Inspired by the USSR Constitution. **Non-justiciable** — no penalty for breach, no court enforcement. Originally **ten duties**; the **eleventh was added by the 86th Amendment, 2002** ([source](https://vajiramandravi.com/upsc-exam/fundamental-duties/)).

> **It shall be the duty of every citizen of India:**
> (a) to abide by the Constitution and respect its ideals and institutions, the National Flag and the National Anthem;
> (b) to cherish and follow the noble ideals which inspired our national struggle for freedom;
> (c) to uphold and protect the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India;
> (d) to defend the country and render national service when called upon to do so;
> (e) to promote harmony and the spirit of common brotherhood amongst all the people of India transcending religious, linguistic and regional or sectional diversities; to renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women;
> (f) to value and preserve the rich heritage of our composite culture;
> (g) to protect and improve the natural environment including forests, lakes, rivers and wildlife, and to have compassion for living creatures;
> (h) to develop the scientific temper, humanism and the spirit of inquiry and reform;
> (i) to safeguard public property and to abjure violence;
> (j) to strive towards excellence in all spheres of individual and collective activity;
> **(k) — added 2002 — who is a parent or guardian, to provide opportunities for education to his child or, as the case may be, ward between the age of six and fourteen years.**

## 4 · A quick map of the three lists

<div class="cards">
  <div class="card"><h4>Fundamental Rights</h4><p><strong>Enforceable.</strong> If the State violates one, you can go straight to the Supreme Court under Article 32 or to the High Court under Article 226.</p></div>
  <div class="card"><h4>Directive Principles</h4><p><strong>Not enforceable.</strong> They guide State policy. Courts will, however, read DPSPs alongside FRs when interpreting ambiguous statutes — and the balance between the two is itself a basic structure feature.</p></div>
  <div class="card"><h4>Fundamental Duties</h4><p><strong>No penalty.</strong> They are moral obligations on citizens. Some, such as protecting public property, are reflected in statutes that <em>are</em> enforceable; the Article 51A list itself is not.</p></div>
</div>
