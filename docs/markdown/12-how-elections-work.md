# How an Indian Election Actually Runs

<p class="lede">From the moment the Election Commission tweets a date, a 6-to-8 week machinery whirs into motion across the country: notifications go out, nominations open, the Model Code clamps down on government largesse, ~1 million polling booths get equipped with EVMs and VVPATs, ~1.5 crore election workers move into position, and ~969 million voters become eligible to elect their MPs. This chapter is the operating manual.</p>

### 1.1 The Model Code of Conduct

The **Model Code of Conduct (MCC)** takes effect the **moment the schedule is announced** and stays in force until results are declared. It restricts:

- **Government and ministers** — no announcement of new schemes, no transfers of officials, no use of official machinery or funds for political campaigning, no use of public funds for ruling-party advertising.
- **Campaigners** — no communal or caste-based appeals; no use of places of worship for campaigning; no transport of voters within a polling-station radius; no display of campaign material within 100 m of a polling station.
- **48-hour silence period** (Section 126, RPA 1951) — no public meeting, procession, or election matter via TV/cinematograph in the last 48 hours before poll closes.

The MCC is famously **not a statute**. It is a consensual code that the ECI enforces under Art. 324's plenary powers via **moral sanction or censure** ([source](https://thewire.in/politics/election-commission-model-code-of-conduct)). Certain breaches — bribery, intimidation, impersonation, "corrupt practices" under Section 123 RPA — are independently prosecutable.

### 1.2 Phased polling

General elections are conducted in **multiple phases** so that Central Armed Police Forces (CAPF) can be physically moved between states. The **2024 Lok Sabha election ran over 7 phases from 19 April to 1 June 2024**, with counting on **4 June 2024** ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Indian_general_election)).

## 2 · Polling infrastructure (2024 baseline)

| Metric | 2024 General Election |
|---:|:---|
| **Polling stations** | ~1.048 million |
| **EVMs deployed** | ~5.5 million |
| **VVPATs paired** | ~5.5 million |
| **Election workers + security** | ~15 million |
| **Total electors** | ~969 million |
| **Total votes cast** | ~642 million |
| **National turnout** | 65.79 % |

The ECI's stated norm is that **no voter should have to travel more than 2 km to a polling station** ([source](https://icfs.org.uk/a-look-inside-the-2024-indian-elections/)).

In **August 2024**, the ECI raised the **maximum electors per polling station from 1,200 to 1,500** — to reduce booth counts and CAPF requirement. The decision was challenged before the Supreme Court ([source](https://www.livelaw.in/top-stories/supreme-court-plea-filed-against-eci-decision-to-increase-number-of-electors-per-polling-station-from-1200-to-1500-273536)).

## 3 · EVMs and VVPATs

### 3.1 The EVM journey

- **May 1982** — EVMs first used in **50 of 123 booths** of the **Paravur Assembly bye-election, Kerala**.
- **1984** — Supreme Court struck down the use as the law then mandated paper ballots.
- **1989/1992** — Parliament amended the RPA and the Conduct of Election Rules to legitimise EVMs.
- **1998 onwards** — EVMs phased back in.
- **2004 General Election** — **all-EVM polling** for the first time nationwide.

An EVM has two units: a **Ballot Unit (BU)** with candidate names and party symbols, on which the voter presses a button, and a **Control Unit (CU)** held by the polling officer that records the vote ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voting_in_India)).

### 3.2 The VVPAT layer

The **Voter-Verifiable Paper Audit Trail (VVPAT)** prints a paper slip with the voter's choice, visible through a transparent window for ~7 seconds, then drops into a sealed box. It was **mandated by the Supreme Court on 8 October 2013** (***Subramanian Swamy v. ECI***) and **paired with every EVM by the 2019 General Election**.

On **8 April 2019** (***N. Chandrababu Naidu v. UoI***), the Supreme Court raised mandatory VVPAT-slip verification from **1 polling station per Assembly Constituency** to **5 randomly chosen polling stations per AC** ([source](https://www.iasparliament.com/current-affairs/daily-news/supreme-court-order-on-vvpat-verification)).

In April 2024, the Supreme Court declined a petition for 100 % VVPAT verification but added safeguards: candidates losing by a small margin can request a re-verification of the EVM and VVPAT memory.

## 4 · The voter

### 4.1 EPIC and the electoral roll

- **EPIC** — Electors Photo Identity Card. Issued by the ECI.
- **Electoral Roll** — a polling-station-wise list of all eligible voters. Maintained by the **Electoral Registration Officer (ERO)** with help from the **Booth Level Officer (BLO)**.

### 4.2 Forms you need to know

| Form | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Form 6** | Apply for a new voter ID / first-time registration |
| **Form 6A** | Application by an overseas elector |
| **Form 7** | Objection to inclusion / application for deletion |
| **Form 8** | Correction of entries; transposition to another constituency; mark as PwD |

### 4.3 Postal ballots and ETPBS

- **Service voters** (armed forces, paramilitary, election-duty staff, government officials posted abroad) vote via the **Electronically Transmitted Postal Ballot System (ETPBS)** — a blank postal ballot is electronically delivered, printed, marked, and physically returned.
- **Home voting**: For the **2024 General Election**, the ECI rolled out home voting **nationwide** for citizens aged **85 and above** and **persons with disabilities** (≥ 40 % benchmark). Eligible voters file **Form 12D** within 5 days of notification, a polling team visits with the ballot, and the entire process is video-recorded ([source](https://ddnews.gov.in/en/election-commission-introduces-home-voting-for-elderly-and-persons-with-disabilities/)).

### 4.4 NOTA

The "**None of the Above**" button on the EVM was directed by the **Supreme Court on 27 September 2013** in ***PUCL v. Union of India*** (2013) 10 SCC 1. The Court held that **secrecy of the ballot must extend to those choosing to vote for nobody** and that NOTA may pressure parties to nominate cleaner candidates ([source](https://indconlawphil.wordpress.com/2013/09/28/pucl-v-union-of-india-the-supreme-court-and-negative-voting/)).

NOTA does **not** trigger a re-poll even if it wins the highest share — the candidate with the most votes (other than NOTA) is still elected. Some states (e.g., Maharashtra, Haryana, Puducherry, Delhi) have adopted "**Right to Reject**"-like rules at the panchayat/municipal level.

## 5 · Expenditure ceilings — and the loophole

In **January 2022**, the ECI **revised candidate expenditure ceilings** ([source](https://old.eci.gov.in/files/file/13928-limits-of-candidate%E2%80%99s-expenses-enhanced/)):

| Office | Larger states | Smaller states |
|---|---:|---:|
| Lok Sabha | **₹95 lakh** (up from ₹70L) | **₹75 lakh** (up from ₹54L) |
| Assembly | **₹40 lakh** (up from ₹28L) | **₹28 lakh** (up from ₹20L) |

Critically, **there is no statutory cap on what a political party itself can spend** — only on individual candidates. This is the central financial loophole in Indian campaign-finance law.

The Centre for Media Studies estimated the **2024 Lok Sabha election** cost around **₹1,00,000 crore** (some experts pegged it as high as ₹1.35 lakh crore) — making it **the most expensive election in world history** ([source](https://adrindia.org/content/2024-ls-polls-pegged-costliest-ever-expenditure-may-touch-rs-135-lakh-crore-expert)).

## 6 · Election finance and the bonds case

The **Electoral Bond Scheme** was announced in the 2017–18 Budget and notified on **2 January 2018**. Donors could buy interest-free bearer bonds from SBI in denominations of ₹1,000 / ₹10,000 / ₹1 lakh / ₹10 lakh / ₹1 crore and deposit them with any registered party — with **donor identity hidden** from the recipient and the public. The 2018 scheme also **removed the 7.5 % cap** on corporate political donations (calculated as average of three preceding years' net profit).

On **15 February 2024**, a **5-judge Supreme Court Constitution Bench led by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud** **unanimously struck down the scheme** as violative of the **voter's right to information under Article 19(1)(a)** ([source](https://www.scconline.com/blog/post/2024/02/15/electoral-bonds-violative-right-to-information-art-191a-constitution-supreme-court-strikes-down-unconstitutional/)). The Court also struck down the Companies Act amendment that had removed the 7.5 % cap, restoring the pre-2017 regime.

On **11 March 2024**, the Court ordered SBI to hand over all bond data by next-day close of business; the **ECI published the data on its website on 15 March 2024**, covering bonds encashed between 12 April 2019 and 24 January 2024. The disclosure revealed:

- **BJP** was by far the largest beneficiary, followed by **TMC, INC, BRS** and others.
- **Top corporate donors** included Megha Engineering & Infrastructure (MEIL), Vedanta, Bharti Airtel, Grasim, Quick Supply Chain (linked to the Adani group).

No successor transparency mechanism has been legislated. The election ecosystem reverted to **electoral trusts** and direct corporate giving (subject to the 7.5 % cap and disclosure regime).

After the poll closes:

1. **EVM sealed** in the presence of polling agents.
2. **Transported** under armed escort to **strongrooms** at the counting centre.
3. On **counting day**, EVMs from each AC are randomly assigned to counting tables.
4. **Control Units** are activated; totals are read out.
5. **VVPAT verification** for 5 random booths per AC (post-2019).
6. Final tallies declared by the **Returning Officer**.

## 8 · Disqualifications — Section 8 of the RPA, 1951

A candidate is **disqualified** if convicted of certain offences:

- **Section 8(1)** — Conviction under specified statutes (e.g., promoting enmity, bribery, undue influence at elections) → disqualified for 6 years from release.
- **Section 8(2)** — Conviction relating to hoarding/profiteering/food adulteration, or under FCRA, NDPS, TADA → disqualification varies.
- **Section 8(3)** — **Conviction for any offence with sentence of imprisonment ≥ 2 years** → disqualified from the date of conviction, and for a further 6 years after release.

In ***Lily Thomas v. Union of India* (10 July 2013)**, the Supreme Court struck down **Section 8(4)** of the RPA — which had allowed sitting legislators a 3-month window to appeal a conviction without losing their seat. Today, a sitting MP/MLA convicted of a 2+ year sentence is **disqualified immediately**. (Rahul Gandhi's 2023 disqualification after a defamation conviction, later stayed by the Supreme Court, was a high-profile application.)

## 9 · The ECI's powers under stress

Three flashpoints in 2024–26 illustrate how the ECI's autonomy is being tested:

1. **CEC Act 2023 — selection panel composition.** The replacement of the CJI with a Union Cabinet Minister has been called a "**dilution of independence**" by petitioners and several constitutional commentators. SC interim stay refused; final hearing pending.
2. **Voters-per-booth.** August 2024 raise from 1,200 → 1,500 challenged before the SC for diluting the "no more than 2 km" promise and increasing waiting times.
3. **Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of electoral rolls in West Bengal (2025–26)**. Approximately **9 million entries (~12 % of the roll)** were deleted ahead of the April–May 2026 Assembly election. The TMC alleged mass disenfranchisement of genuine voters; the BJP defended the SIR as the removal of bogus entries and illegal migrants. Several petitions are pending before the Calcutta HC and the Supreme Court.
