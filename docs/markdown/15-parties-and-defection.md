# Political Parties & the Anti-Defection Law

<p class="lede">Political parties are not mentioned in the original Constitution — yet they are how modern Indian democracy organises itself. The Election Commission has the power to <em>register</em> them under the Representation of the People Act, 1951, and to <em>recognise</em> them as "National" or "State" parties under the Symbols Order, 1968. The Tenth Schedule, added in 1985 and tightened in 2003, tries (with mixed success) to make a party's MPs and MLAs actually act like a party.</p>

## 1 · Registration under Section 29A, RPA 1951

A political party in India must be **registered with the Election Commission** under **Section 29A of the Representation of the People Act, 1951**. The process ([source](https://eci.gov.in/files/file/11407-registration-of-political-parties-under-section-29a-of-the-representation-of-the-people-act-1951-issue-of-additional-guidelines-regarding/)):

1. **Application within 30 days of formation**, with name, address, office-bearers, membership and constitution.
2. **Publication of the proposed name** in two national + two local dailies, inviting objections within **30 days**.
3. **Affidavits** from at least 100 members.
4. A **demand draft** for ₹10,000 fee.

**Registration is mandatory** to:
- Claim **Section 13A** Income-tax benefits.
- Receive an allotted **symbol** for elections.

**Registration alone does NOT confer "recognised" status.** A registered-but-unrecognised party must pick a **free symbol** from the ECI's pool at each election.

A **Political Parties Registration Tracking Management System (PPRTMS)** was launched in January 2020 to allow SMS/email tracking of applications.

## 2 · Recognition under the Symbols Order, 1968

The Election Commission's **Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order, 1968** governs party recognition.

### 2.1 National Party — criteria (any one)

A party is recognised as **National** if it meets any one of:

1. Secures **at least 6 % of valid votes** in Lok Sabha or Assembly elections in **four or more states**, AND wins at least **4 Lok Sabha seats**; OR
2. Wins at least **2 % of total Lok Sabha seats (currently 11)** from **at least three different states**; OR
3. Is recognised as a **State Party in at least four states**.

### 2.2 State Party — criteria (any one)

1. **6 % valid votes** in a State Assembly election + at least **2 Assembly seats**; OR
2. **6 % valid votes** in a Lok Sabha election from the state + at least **1 LS seat**; OR
3. **3 % of total Assembly seats or 3 seats** (whichever higher); OR
4. **8 % of total valid votes polled in the state** in either election.

### 2.3 Benefits of recognition

- **Reserved (exclusive) symbol** — national (for National parties) or within the state (for State parties).
- **Free broadcast time** on state-run Doordarshan and All India Radio during election campaigns.
- **Free copies of electoral rolls**.
- Allowance of **40 "star campaigners"** (versus 20 for unrecognised registered parties).
- Consultation by the ECI in setting poll dates and electoral rules.

### 2.4 Current National Parties (post-2023 review)

The ECI's **10 April 2023 review** updated the list of National Parties. As of mid-2026:

| National Party | Symbol | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP)** | Lotus | National since 1980 |
| **Indian National Congress (INC)** | Hand | Oldest party (1885); National since inception |
| **Communist Party of India (Marxist) — CPI(M)** | Hammer, Sickle, Star | Split from CPI in 1964 |
| **Bahujan Samaj Party (BSP)** | Elephant | Founded 1984 |
| **National People's Party (NPP)** | Book | First North-East-based National Party (recognised 2019) |
| **Aam Aadmi Party (AAP)** | Broom | Recognised as National Party in April 2023 |

The same April 2023 review **stripped national status from CPI, NCP and TMC** — for failing to meet the 6 % / 4 LS seats criteria across enough states.

### 2.5 Symbols: reserved vs free

Recognised parties have a **reserved symbol**. Unrecognised registered parties and independents pick from a **pool of free symbols** at the time of nomination. Common free symbols: pressure cooker, telephone, cap, slate, jug, etc. The ECI's symbol pool now includes hundreds of items, regularly refreshed.

## 3 · Party finance

### 3.1 Section 13A, Income-tax Act 1961

A registered political party is **fully exempt** from income tax on:

- Income from house property
- Income from other sources (incl. voluntary contributions)
- Capital gains

— provided the party:

1. Maintains books of account.
2. Audits accounts.
3. **Records the name and address of every donor giving above ₹20,000** (other than via electoral bonds during their existence).
4. **Accepts no donation above ₹2,000 except by account-payee cheque, bank draft, prescribed electronic mode or electoral bond**.
5. Files its income-tax return on time ([source](https://tax2win.in/guide/section-13a-of-income-tax-act)).

### 3.2 Section 29C, RPA 1951 — annual contribution report

The treasurer of a political party must submit an **annual contribution report** to the ECI listing all donations above ₹20,000. The report must be filed before the due date of the income-tax return; failure to do so means loss of Section 13A benefits ([source](https://adrindia.org/sites/default/files/FAQ%20-%20ITR%20of%20Political%20Parties.pdf)).

### 3.3 Star campaigners — Section 77, RPA 1951

The **travel expenses of "leaders of a political party" — star campaigners — are NOT counted against the contesting candidate's expense limit**.

- **Recognised party**: up to **40 star campaigners**.
- **Unrecognised registered party**: up to **20 star campaigners**.

But the immunity is conditional: if a star campaigner **solicits votes for a specific candidate** or **shares the dais** with the candidate, the meeting cost is apportioned to that candidate. If the candidate **travels in the same vehicle/helicopter** as the star campaigner, **50 % of travel costs** are apportioned ([source](https://www.shankariasparliament.com/current-affairs/rules-for-star-campaigners)).

## 4 · The Anti-Defection Law (Tenth Schedule)

### 4.1 Why it exists

In the 1960s and 70s, Indian politics had a phenomenon called "**Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram**" (from a 1967 episode in Haryana where MLA Gaya Lal switched parties three times in a single fortnight). Governments rose and fell on the back of MLA defections. The Anti-Defection Law was the response.

### 4.2 The 52nd Amendment, 1985

The **Constitution (Fifty-second Amendment) Act, 1985** inserted the **Tenth Schedule** into the Constitution, prescribing **disqualification on the ground of defection**.

### 4.3 Grounds for disqualification

A legislator is disqualified for:

1. **Voluntarily giving up membership** of the party on whose ticket they were elected — including by **conduct** (e.g., joining another party's rally, accepting ministership in another party's government), not only by formal resignation. The SC clarified in *Ravi S. Naik v. UoI* (1994) that "voluntarily giving up" need not equate to formal resignation.
2. **Voting or abstaining contrary to a party whip**, without prior permission, unless condoned within **15 days**.
3. **An independent elected legislator joining any political party** after election.
4. **A nominated member joining a political party** after **6 months** from taking the seat (a nominated member can join their original party within 6 months without consequence).

### 4.4 The 91st Amendment, 2003

The **Constitution (Ninety-first Amendment) Act, 2003** tightened the law significantly:

- **Removed the "split" exception**: Previously a defection by **one-third** of a legislature party was exempt as a "split." The split exception was **deleted entirely**.
- **Retained only the "merger" exception**: A defection is now exempt only if the **two-thirds** of the legislature party agree to merge with another party, **and the original party formally merges**.
- **Cap on ministers** added (Articles 75(1A) and 164(1A) — 15 % of LS/Assembly).
- **Disqualified member bar**: A member disqualified under the Tenth Schedule cannot be appointed as Minister or hold any remunerative political post (Articles 75(1B), 164(1B)).

### 4.5 Who decides? The Speaker / Chairperson

The **Speaker** (in the Lok Sabha / Vidhan Sabha) or **Chairperson** (in the Rajya Sabha / Vidhan Parishad) decides disqualification petitions. The decision is **subject to judicial review** per ***Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu* (1992)** — a 5-judge Constitution Bench upheld the Tenth Schedule's validity (3:2) but struck down Paragraph 7 (which had ousted judicial review) for want of state ratification under Article 368(2). The Speaker, when adjudicating, acts as a **Tribunal exercising judicial power**.

### 4.6 The "reasonable period" — *Keisham Meghachandra Singh* (2020)

In ***Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Hon'ble Speaker, Manipur Legislative Assembly* (21 January 2020)**, the Supreme Court held that disqualification petitions before a Speaker should ordinarily be **decided within three months** ([source](https://www.livelaw.in/top-stories/speaker-should-decide-on-disqualification-within-3-months-impartial-tribunal-needed-under-10th-schedule-sc-151868)). The Court also urged Parliament to consider whether disqualification adjudication should be entrusted to a **partisan Speaker**, suggesting an "**independent tribunal**" of retired judges instead. The 3-month limit is **judicial guidance — not statutorily binding**.

## 5 · Three recent landmark cases

### 5.1 Karnataka 2019 — 17 MLAs

A bench of Justices N.V. Ramana, Sanjiv Khanna and Krishna Murari upheld Speaker K.R. Ramesh Kumar's disqualification of **17 rebel Congress and JDS MLAs** after the fall of the H.D. Kumaraswamy government, but **set aside the Speaker's order barring them from contesting until the end of the Assembly term** — holding that the **Speaker has no power to prescribe the duration of disqualification** ([source](https://www.livelaw.in/top-stories/-sc-upholds-karnataka-speakers-disqualification-of-17-mlas-reduces-term--149710)). The disqualified MLAs were free to contest the subsequent by-elections.

### 5.2 Maharashtra Shiv Sena — *Subhash Desai v. Principal Secretary, Governor of Maharashtra* (11 May 2023)

A five-judge Constitution Bench held that the **Governor's decision to call a floor test against Uddhav Thackeray was unjustified** in the absence of objective material indicating loss of majority. But the Court **refused to restore the Thackeray government** because Thackeray had resigned before facing the floor test — leaving the Governor justified in inviting **Eknath Shinde** to form government ([source](https://www.casemine.com/commentary/in/clarifying-anti-defection-and-party-symbol-allotment:-insights-from-subhash-desai-v.-principal-secretary,-governor-of-maharashtra-(2023-insc-516)/view)).

The judgment also clarified that the **Speaker — not the ECI's symbol-allocation order — decides who is the "real" legislative-party** for Tenth Schedule purposes.

On **10 January 2024**, Maharashtra Speaker Rahul Narwekar held the **Shinde faction to be the "real Shiv Sena"**, declining to disqualify its 37 MLAs. The ECI had earlier (February 2023) allotted the original "bow and arrow" symbol to the Shinde faction.

### 5.3 NCP split (July 2023)

**Ajit Pawar** broke away in July 2023 with the support of **41 of 53 NCP MLAs**. The **Election Commission on 6 February 2024 allotted the "clock" symbol to the Ajit Pawar faction**.

On **15 February 2024**, Speaker Rahul Narwekar held the **Ajit Pawar faction to be the "real NCP"**, reasoning that defying party founder Sharad Pawar's wishes did not constitute defection — it was "internal dissent" ([source](https://www.theweek.in/news/india/2024/02/15/ajit-pawars-faction-real-ncp-mlas-cant-disqualified-maharashtra-speaker.html)).

### 5.4 Telangana 2024–26

Between March and April 2024, **ten BRS MLAs defected to the ruling Congress**. Speaker Gaddam Prasad Kumar dismissed disqualification petitions, citing "lack of conclusive documentary or legally sustainable evidence." The Supreme Court in **July 2025 directed the Speaker to decide within three months**; **contempt notices were issued in November 2025** when the deadline lapsed; a further three-week extension was granted in February 2026 ([source](https://www.deccanherald.com/india/telangana/supreme-court-directs-telangana-assembly-speaker-to-decide-on-disqualification-plea-against-10-brs-mlas-who-defected-to-congress-3658409)).

## 6 · What the Tenth Schedule has and hasn't done

| Achieved | Not achieved |
|---|---|
| Reduced casual floor-crossing | Eliminated mass defections (Shinde, Ajit Pawar) |
| Made "split exception" abuse impossible | Made "merger exception" + Speaker rulings the new escape hatch |
| Brought defections under judicial review | Created timely, impartial adjudication |
| Tied minister-appointments to membership | Prevented strategic disqualifications |

The recurring critique — voiced by Speaker Subhash Kashyap, by the Dinesh Goswami Committee, by NCRWC 2002, and by *Keisham Meghachandra Singh* — is that the **Speaker is structurally unable to be impartial** because they remain a politician of one party. The proposal: **transfer the decision to an independent constitutional authority** (a tribunal of retired judges, or the ECI itself). No such reform has been enacted.

## 7 · Why parties pick "winnable" candidates — Vaishnav's thesis

Milan Vaishnav's **When Crime Pays: Money and Muscle in Indian Politics** (Yale University Press, 2017) — see Chapter 16 for the data — argues that parties **rationally** select candidates with criminal records in many contexts: such candidates are **self-financing** (they bring their own money) and **winnable** (in fragmented multi-party contests, they offer co-ethnic protection and "delivery"). The Tenth Schedule and the recognition rules act on parties — but parties' **candidate-selection** logic remains driven by winnability above all else.
