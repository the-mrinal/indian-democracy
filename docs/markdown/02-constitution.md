# The Constitution: Birth, Structure, Soul

<p class="lede">The Indian Constitution is the longest written constitution in the world — roughly 145,000 words, 448 articles, 25 Parts, 12 Schedules — drafted over nearly three years by a directly-deliberative Constituent Assembly and amended 106 times since. It is the source code from which every elected office, every public power, and every fundamental right in India is generated.</p>

## 1 · The Constituent Assembly (1946–1949)

India's Constitution was drafted by the **Constituent Assembly**, set up under the **Cabinet Mission Plan, 1946**. The Assembly first sat on **9 December 1946** in the Constitution Hall (now the Central Hall of the old Parliament building) with **207 members** in attendance; eventual full strength was **389 members** — 292 from British Indian provinces, 93 from princely states, and 4 from chief commissioner provinces. After Partition (15 August 1947), the membership shrank to **299**. The Assembly sat for **167 days of plenary debate over a period of 2 years, 11 months, and 18 days** ([source](https://www.constitutionofindia.net/about/)).

The key figures were:

- **Dr. Rajendra Prasad** — President of the Constituent Assembly (later India's first President).
- **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** — Chairman of the **Drafting Committee** (7 members) and chief architect of the Constitution.
- **Jawaharlal Nehru** — moved the **Objectives Resolution** on 13 December 1946 that became the philosophical seed of the Preamble.
- **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel** — Chairman of the Advisory Committee on Fundamental Rights, Minorities and Tribal Areas.
- **Sir B.N. Rau** — Constitutional Adviser to the Assembly; prepared the initial draft.
- **K.M. Munshi**, **Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar**, **Gopalaswami Ayyangar**, **Mohammad Saadulla**, **N. Madhava Rao**, **T.T. Krishnamachari** — drafting committee colleagues.

The Constitution was **adopted on 26 November 1949** (now celebrated as **Samvidhan Divas / Constitution Day**) and **came into force on 26 January 1950** — Republic Day. The 26 January date was chosen deliberately: on the same day in 1930, the Indian National Congress at its Lahore Session had declared **Purna Swaraj** (complete independence). Adopting the Constitution on that anniversary tied the new Republic to the freedom struggle.

## 2 · Borrowed but not copied

Ambedkar said in the Assembly that he had "ransacked all the known Constitutions of the world." The result is an Indian synthesis built on selectively borrowed features ([source](https://www.studyiq.com/articles/borrowed-features-of-the-indian-constitution/)):

| Source | Borrowed feature |
|---|---|
| **United Kingdom** | Parliamentary government, rule of law, single citizenship, Speaker's office, cabinet system |
| **United States** | Fundamental Rights, judicial review, impeachment of the President, removal of judges, Vice-President's role |
| **Ireland** | Directive Principles of State Policy, method of presidential election, nomination of Rajya Sabha members |
| **Canada** | Federation with a strong centre, residuary powers vested in the Union |
| **Australia** | Concurrent List, freedom of trade and commerce, joint sitting of Parliament |
| **Weimar Germany** | Suspension of Fundamental Rights during a national emergency |
| **Japan** | Procedure established by law (used in Article 21) |
| **USSR** | Fundamental Duties, ideal of "Justice — social, economic, political" in the Preamble |
| **South Africa** | Amendment procedure (Art. 368), election of Rajya Sabha members |
| **France** | Republic, ideals of liberty, equality, fraternity |

The most direct intellectual ancestor is actually domestic: the **Government of India Act, 1935**, from which the Constitution inherited its federal scheme, the offices of Governor and Speaker, the public service commissions, emergency provisions, and a great deal of its administrative grammar.

## 3 · The shape of the document

The Constitution **originally had 395 Articles in 22 Parts and 8 Schedules**. As of June 2026 it contains **approximately 448 Articles** (with renumbering up to ~470 after insertions like 21A, 31A–C, 35A, 243A–ZT, 338A–B), **25 substantive Parts**, and **12 Schedules**, after **106 constitutional amendments** ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_India); [source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_amendments_of_the_Constitution_of_India)).

### The 12 Schedules

1. **First** — Names of States and UTs and their territorial extent.
2. **Second** — Salaries/emoluments of President, Governors, Speaker, Judges, CAG.
3. **Third** — Forms of oaths and affirmations for constitutional office-holders.
4. **Fourth** — Allocation of seats in the Rajya Sabha to States and UTs.
5. **Fifth** — Administration and control of Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes.
6. **Sixth** — Administration of Tribal Areas in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram.
7. **Seventh** — Union List (98 subjects), State List (59), Concurrent List (47); residuary powers with Parliament (Art. 248).
8. **Eighth** — 22 officially recognised languages (Bodo, Dogri, Maithili, Santhali added by **92nd Amdt, 2003**).
9. **Ninth** — Acts and Regulations protected from judicial review (inserted by **1st Amdt, 1951**; this protection limited post-*I.R. Coelho* 2007 for laws inserted after 24 April 1973).
10. **Tenth** — Anti-defection provisions (**52nd Amdt, 1985**).
11. **Eleventh** — 29 subjects for Panchayats (**73rd Amdt, 1992**).
12. **Twelfth** — 18 subjects for Municipalities (**74th Amdt, 1992**).

## 4 · The Preamble

> *"WE, THE PEOPLE OF INDIA, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a **SOVEREIGN SOCIALIST SECULAR DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC** and to secure to all its citizens: **JUSTICE**, social, economic and political; **LIBERTY** of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship; **EQUALITY** of status and of opportunity; and to promote among them all **FRATERNITY** assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation; IN OUR CONSTITUENT ASSEMBLY this twenty-sixth day of November, 1949, do HEREBY ADOPT, ENACT AND GIVE TO OURSELVES THIS CONSTITUTION."*

The Preamble has been amended **only once** — by the **42nd Amendment, 18 December 1976** (during the Emergency, the Indira Gandhi government), which inserted the words **"Socialist"** and **"Secular"** between "Sovereign" and "Democratic", and replaced "unity of the Nation" with "**unity and integrity of the Nation**" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forty-second_Amendment_of_the_Constitution_of_India)). In **Dr. Balram Singh v. Union of India** (2024), the Supreme Court upheld these insertions, holding that they were consistent with the existing ideals of the Preamble and did not violate the Basic Structure ([source](https://visionias.in/current-affairs/monthly-magazine/2024-12-17/polity-and-governance/socialist-secular-in-the-preamble)).

The Preamble was held in *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973) to be **part of the Constitution** and to embody its basic philosophy — though not itself enforceable.

## 5 · The Basic Structure Doctrine

Article 368 gives Parliament the power to amend the Constitution. But can Parliament amend it *out of existence*? Three landmark judgments answered:

- ***Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973)** — A 13-judge bench by a razor-thin **7:6 majority** held that Parliament can amend any provision of the Constitution but **cannot destroy its "basic structure."** This is the doctrine.
- ***Minerva Mills v. Union of India* (1980)** — Struck down parts of the 42nd Amendment that had sought to immunise constitutional amendments from judicial review; held that **judicial review** and **the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles** are themselves part of the basic structure ([source](https://padhai.ai/blogs-padhai/basic-structure-doctrine-of-the-constitution-upsc-exam)).
- ***I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu* (2007)** — A 9-judge bench held that **even Ninth Schedule laws inserted after 24 April 1973** (the date of *Kesavananda*) are open to judicial review if they violate the basic structure or fundamental rights.

Identified basic features include: **supremacy of the Constitution, secularism, federalism, democracy, republic form of government, rule of law, separation of powers, free and fair elections, judicial review, and the principle that the dignity of the individual is paramount.**

<div class="callout">
  <strong>Why this matters</strong>
  Without the Basic Structure doctrine, a Parliament with a 2/3 majority could (for example) abolish judicial review or extend its own tenure indefinitely. The doctrine puts an outer limit on constitutional amendment power — and is itself, ironically, judge-made.
</div>

## 6 · Amendment procedure (Article 368)

Amendments come in **three flavours**, depending on what is being changed ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amendment_of_the_Constitution_of_India)):

| Mode | What it amends | Majority required |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple majority** | Provisions outside Art. 368 — e.g., Art. 4 (admission/formation of new states), Art. 169 (abolition/creation of Legislative Councils), parts of the 5th/6th Schedules | Simple majority in each House |
| **Special majority** | Most amendments (e.g., Fundamental Rights, DPSP) | Majority of total membership of each House **AND** ≥ 2/3 of members present and voting |
| **Special majority + state ratification** | Federal provisions — presidential election (Arts 54–55), executive powers (Arts 73, 162), Supreme Court / High Courts, Schedule VII (Union/State/Concurrent Lists), representation of states in Parliament, Article 368 itself | Above + ratification by ≥ ½ of state legislatures |

## 7 · Six recent amendments worth knowing

- **101st (2016)** — Goods and Services Tax; created Article 279A (GST Council); GST effective 1 July 2017.
- **102nd (2018)** — Granted **constitutional status to the National Commission for Backward Classes** via Articles 338B and 342A.
- **103rd (2019)** — **10 % EWS reservation** in education and public employment (income ceiling ₹8 lakh p.a.); upheld by SC in *Janhit Abhiyan* (2022).
- **104th (effective 25 January 2020)** — Extended **SC/ST reservation in Lok Sabha and State Assemblies until 25 January 2030**; simultaneously **abolished the Anglo-Indian nominated seats** (so LS strength is now 543, not 545).
- **105th (2021)** — Restored states' power to identify their own Socially & Educationally Backward Classes (SEBCs), reversing the SC's reading of the 102nd Amendment in the *Maratha quota* case.
- **106th (2023) — Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam** — **33 % reservation for women in the Lok Sabha, State Legislative Assemblies, and the Delhi Legislative Assembly** via new Articles 330A, 332A and 334A. Lok Sabha 454–2 (20 Sept 2023); Rajya Sabha 214–0 (21 Sept 2023). **Operational only after fresh census + delimitation** — earliest application likely **2029 General Election** ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Hundred_and_Sixth_Amendment_of_the_Constitution_of_India)).

<div class="callout fact">
  <strong>The Constitution as a living document</strong>
  106 amendments in 75 years means India amends its Constitution roughly every 8 months on average. Compare the United States: 27 amendments in 235 years (one every ~9 years). Indian constitutional design is engineered for amendability — but the Basic Structure doctrine puts a non-textual ceiling on what can be amended away.
</div>

## 8 · The next constitutional moment

Three issues are converging on the **2027–2029 window**:

1. **Delimitation.** The state-wise allocation of Lok Sabha seats was frozen on the **1971 census** by the 42nd Amendment (1976) and the freeze was extended to "the first census after 2026" by the **84th Amendment (2001)**. The first post-2026 census is scheduled for **1 March 2027**. The redistribution that follows is politically explosive — high-fertility northern states (UP, Bihar) would gain seats at the expense of low-fertility southern states (TN, Kerala, Karnataka, AP).
2. **Women's reservation.** The 106th Amendment's 33 % quota for women can only kick in after the post-2026 delimitation — earliest application is the 2029 General Election.
3. **A failed 131st Amendment Bill (2026).** Bills tabled on 16 April 2026 sought to enlarge the Lok Sabha and operationalise both delimitation and women's reservation; the **Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026 failed to get the required two-thirds majority** (298–230, well short of the 352 needed). The status quo on seat allocation therefore continues at the time of writing.
