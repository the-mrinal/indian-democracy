# Introduction: India as a Democracy

<p class="lede">India is the world's most populous democracy: ~1.4 billion citizens, ~969 million registered voters as of the 2024 General Election, organised across one Union, 28 States and 8 Union Territories, voting at over a million polling booths in elections run by an autonomous Election Commission. The system is large, layered, and noisy — but its skeleton is surprisingly clean once you see it.</p>

## What kind of state is India, exactly?

The **Preamble** of the Constitution describes India as a "**Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, Republic**". Each adjective is a load-bearing claim:

- **Sovereign** — the state's authority is not derived from any external power. India is not a dominion of the British Crown; it is its own source of legal authority.
- **Socialist** — added in 1976 by the 42nd Amendment, in the sense of a *mixed economy* aimed at reducing inequality, not Soviet-style socialism. The Supreme Court has clarified (e.g., *Excel Wear v. Union of India*, 1978) this does not preclude private enterprise.
- **Secular** — also added in 1976. The state has no religion and treats all faiths equally (*sarva dharma sambhava*), which is a different model from the strict French *laïcité*.
- **Democratic** — government by representatives directly or indirectly chosen by the people, on the basis of **universal adult suffrage** (Article 326).
- **Republic** — the head of state is an elected office (the President), not a hereditary monarch.

The four social-political goals follow: **Justice** (social, economic, political), **Liberty** (of thought, expression, belief, faith, worship), **Equality** (of status and opportunity), and **Fraternity** (assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation).

  <div class="diagram-note">
    <strong>India's twist on separation of powers.</strong> In the US model, the three organs are strictly independent. In India's <em>parliamentary</em> (Westminster) model, the <strong>Executive is drawn <em>from</em> the Legislature</strong> — the PM and every Cabinet Minister must be a sitting MP. That makes the Cabinet collectively answerable to the Lok Sabha, but it also fuses two of the three organs at the top. The <strong>Judiciary, however, stays independent</strong> — judges are not drawn from the legislature, and their appointment is controlled by the Collegium of senior judges themselves (see Chapter 10).

## Where authority actually sits

A useful mental model:

| Layer | Legislature | Executive | Head |
|---|---|---|---|
| Union | Parliament (Lok Sabha + Rajya Sabha) | Council of Ministers led by PM | **President** (constitutional head) |
| State | Vidhan Sabha (+ Vidhan Parishad in some states) | State Council of Ministers led by CM | **Governor** (constitutional head) |
| Local — Rural | Gram Sabha → Panchayat (3-tier) | Sarpanch + panchayat | Sarpanch / Pradhan |
| Local — Urban | Municipal Council / Corporation | Mayor + standing committees | Mayor / Commissioner |

The pattern is the same at Union and State level: a **constitutional head** (President / Governor) who acts on the advice of the **real executive** (PM + Cabinet / CM + Cabinet), which is responsible to the **directly elected lower house** (Lok Sabha / Vidhan Sabha).

## The cast of elected offices — preview

You will hear these terms throughout the guide:

- **MP (Lok Sabha)** — Member of Parliament elected directly by voters of one of 543 constituencies via First-Past-The-Post for a 5-year term.
- **MP (Rajya Sabha)** — Member of Parliament elected *indirectly* by elected MLAs of a State via Single Transferable Vote for a 6-year term (1/3 retire every 2 years).
- **MLA** — Member of Legislative Assembly, directly elected by voters of an Assembly Constituency in a State.
- **MLC** — Member of Legislative Council, elected indirectly in the 6 states that still have an Upper House (UP, Maharashtra, Bihar, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana).
- **President** — Head of State, elected indirectly by an Electoral College of all elected MPs + all elected MLAs (with weighted votes) using Single Transferable Vote.
- **Vice-President** — Elected by both Houses of Parliament (elected + nominated MPs) using STV; ex officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha.
- **PM** — Leader of the party/coalition commanding a majority in the Lok Sabha; appointed by the President.
- **CM** — Leader of the party/coalition commanding a majority in a state's Legislative Assembly; appointed by the Governor.
- **Governor** — Appointed by the President; constitutional head of a State.
- **Speaker / Deputy Speaker / Chairman / Deputy Chairman** — Presiding officers of the legislative chambers.
- **Sarpanch / Pradhan** — Elected head of a Gram Panchayat (village).
- **Mayor** — Elected head of a Municipal Corporation/Council.

Each is unpacked in detail in the chapters that follow.

<div class="callout fact">
  <strong>Reality check</strong>
  Most political "power" in day-to-day governance flows through the <strong>Council of Ministers</strong> at the Union and the State level. The President and Governor have largely ceremonial roles, but they hold reserve constitutional powers (e.g., dissolving the lower house, recommending President's Rule under Article 356, granting pardons) that become decisive in moments of crisis.
</div>

## What this guide does <em>not</em> assume

You don't need a background in law, civics or history. You **do** need to be comfortable with the idea that:

1. **Multiple things are true at once.** India is *parliamentary* (executive answers to legislature) **and** *federal* (Centre and States have constitutionally protected powers) **and** *quasi-federal* (the Centre is much stronger than in classic federations like the US). It is a constitutional democracy *and* a noisy electoral one.
2. **The text of the Constitution and the practice of politics are different things.** The text says the Governor acts on the advice of the State Council of Ministers; practice in recent years has shown Governors withholding assent for months. The text caps election spending; practice routinely blows through that cap by orders of magnitude. We will keep flagging the gap.
3. **Numbers and names change.** Seat counts, CEC names, current CJI, who controls which state — these shift. The structure does not.
