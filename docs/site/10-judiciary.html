Anchored in M. Laxmikanth's Indian Polity and Shivam Shankar Singh's How to Win an Indian Election.

India has a single integrated judiciary — one pyramid, not two. The Supreme Court sits at the apex; 25 High Courts (each covering one or more states/UTs) below it; subordinate civil and criminal courts at the district and taluka level. Its job is to interpret the Constitution, settle disputes, and — through judicial review — police the limits of legislative and executive power. The Basic Structure doctrine, the PIL revolution, the Collegium, and the long-running fight over judicial appointments have made this the most institutionally muscular judiciary in the democratic world.

Reading time: ~20 min Pairs with: Laxmikanth, Ch. 26 (SC), 33 (HC), 34 (Subordinate Courts) · D.D. Basu on the Judiciary · Granville Austin (1999), Ch. on the Court

1 · The shape of the pyramid

India's single integrated judicial pyramid — appeals flow upward
SUPREME COURT

Tilak Marg, New Delhi · Articles 124–147 · CJI + 33 judges (max 34, since 2019 amendment)

  • Original (Art 131) — Centre–State and inter-State disputes
  • Writ (Art 32) — enforcement of Fundamental Rights
  • Appellate (Arts 132–134, 136 SLP) — civil, criminal, constitutional appeals from any HC
  • Advisory (Art 143) · Review (Art 137) · Curative (judge-made, 2002)
▲ appeals
HIGH COURTS  ·  25 of them

Articles 214–231 · Chief Justice + judges per state (Allahabad ~160; Sikkim ~3)

  • Writ jurisdiction (Art 226) — for FRs and any other legal right (wider than SC's Art 32)
  • Supervisory (Art 227) over all subordinate courts and tribunals in their territory
  • 5 HCs serve multiple states/UTs (Bombay, Calcutta, Gauhati, Madras, P&H); J&K + Ladakh share one
▲ appeals
DISTRICT & SESSIONS COURTS

Top of the subordinate judiciary in each district

  • District Judge — civil side
  • Sessions Judge — criminal side (often same person)
  • Appointed by the Governor in consultation with the HC (Art 233)
TRIBUNALS

Parts XIV-A, Articles 323A & 323B (added 42nd Amdt, 1976)

  • CAT — service matters; ITAT, NCLT, NGT, AFT, CESTAT
  • Final orders subject to HC writ jurisdiction (L. Chandra Kumar, 1997)
▲ appeals
SUBORDINATE COURTS — CIVIL

Munsif → Civil Judge (Jr Div) → Civil Judge (Sr Div) → Sub-Judge

  • Hear original civil suits up to pecuniary limits set by state law
  • Appeals → District Judge → High Court
SUBORDINATE COURTS — CRIMINAL

Judicial Magistrate (2nd Class) → Judicial Magistrate (1st Class) → Chief JM

  • Try lesser offences; commit serious offences to Sessions Court
  • Appeals → Sessions Court → High Court
Single pyramid, two streams. Unlike the United States (parallel federal & state court systems), India has one integrated judiciary for both Union and State law. Civil and criminal cases each have their own ladder from below, but they merge at the High Court and Supreme Court above.

Unlike the United States (which has dual federal and state court systems running in parallel), India has one judiciary for both Union and State law. Cases originate at the lowest competent court and travel upward by appeal.

2 · The Supreme Court (Articles 124–147)

2.1 Composition

  • Originally 7 judges + CJI in 1950.
  • Strength revised six times since; current maximum is CJI + 33 other judges = 34, set by the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Act, 2019 (source).

2.2 Current Chief Justice (June 2026)

  • Justice Surya Kant — 53rd Chief Justice of India, sworn in by President Droupadi Murmu on 24 November 2025, will serve until 9 February 2027 (source).
  • Predecessors in 2024–25: CJI D.Y. Chandrachud (50th, 2022–10 Nov 2024); Sanjiv Khanna (51st, 11 Nov 2024 – 13 May 2025); B.R. Gavai (52nd, 14 May 2025 – 23 Nov 2025).

2.3 Appointment — the Collegium system

Judges are appointed by the President under Article 124(2) on the recommendation of the Collegium — a creation of judicial precedent, not the Constitution's text:

  • First Judges Case (1981) — S.P. Gupta v. UoI — Executive primacy in appointments.
  • Second Judges Case (1993) — SC Advocates-on-Record Assn. v. UoI — Judicial primacy. Created the Collegium: CJI + 2 senior-most SC judges.
  • Third Judges Case (1998) — In Re: Special Reference No. 1 of 1998 — Expanded Collegium: CJI + 4 senior-most SC judges.

For Supreme Court appointments, the Collegium recommends names to the Government. The Government can return a name once for reconsideration; if the Collegium reiterates, the appointment is binding. For High Court appointments, the relevant HC Collegium (CJ of HC + 2 senior-most HC judges) initiates the proposal, then the SC Collegium evaluates.

2.4 The NJAC affair (2014–2015)

In 2014, Parliament passed the 99th Constitutional Amendment and the NJAC Act to replace the Collegium with a National Judicial Appointments Commission — CJI + 2 senior SC judges + Law Minister + 2 eminent persons (chosen by a panel of PM, CJI, LoP).

In SC Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (2015, "Fourth Judges Case"), a 5-judge bench struck down the 99th Amendment and the NJAC Act by 4:1 (Khehar, Lokur, Goel, Kurian JJ.; Chelameswar J. dissenting). The Court held that judicial primacy in appointments is part of the Basic Structure; the Law Minister's inclusion compromised judicial independence (source).

The Collegium therefore continues — much criticised for opacity but legally entrenched.

2.5 Jurisdiction of the Supreme Court

Jurisdiction Source Used for

Writ (under Original) Art. 32 Enforcement of Fundamental Rights

Appellate — Criminal Art. 134 Death sentence or other prescribed circumstances

2.6 Public Interest Litigation (PIL)

The 1980s saw the Supreme Court relax the traditional rule of locus standi — that only the party directly aggrieved could approach the court. Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer (Mumbai Kamgar Sabha v. Abdulbhai Faizullabhai, 1976) and Justice P.N. Bhagwati (S.P. Gupta v. UoI, 1981) opened the door to "Public Interest Litigation" — letting "any member of the public having sufficient interest" approach the Court on behalf of bonded labour, prisoners, environment, the rights of disadvantaged groups, and structural reform of administration (source).

PIL has produced major reforms (right to legal aid for the poor; environmental jurisprudence; cleaning up the Ganga and the Yamuna; the Vishaka guidelines on sexual harassment; bonded-labour rescues; prison reform). It has also been criticised as judicial overreach and a vehicle for "tourists" litigating in court rather than the polity.

2.7 Removal of judges — Article 124(4)

A Supreme Court (or High Court) judge can be removed only on the grounds of "proved misbehaviour or incapacity":

  1. Motion signed by at least 100 Lok Sabha members or 50 Rajya Sabha members.
  2. If admitted by the Speaker/Chairman, a 3-member committee (a SC judge, a HC Chief Justice, a distinguished jurist) investigates.
  3. Both Houses must pass the motion in the same session, by special majority (majority of total membership AND ≥ 2/3 of members present and voting).
  4. The President issues the removal order.

No Supreme Court judge has ever been removed. The closest case: Justice V. Ramaswami — the Inquiry Committee found him guilty on 11 of 14 charges (excessive carpets, security expenditure as CJ of Punjab & Haryana HC). On 10 May 1993, the Lok Sabha motion got 196 votes "for", 0 "against", and 205 Congress abstentions — failing for want of the required total-membership majority. Ramaswami retired in February 1994 (source).

3 · High Courts (Articles 214–231)

There are 25 High Courts in India as of 2026 — most recently after the bifurcation of the erstwhile Andhra Pradesh HC on 1 January 2019 into separate HCs for Andhra Pradesh (seat: Amaravati) and Telangana (seat: Hyderabad) (source).

3.1 Multi-state High Courts

Five High Courts have multi-State/UT jurisdiction:

High Court Jurisdiction
Bombay HC Maharashtra, Goa, Dadra & Nagar Haveli and Daman & Diu
Calcutta HC West Bengal, Andaman & Nicobar Islands
Gauhati HC Assam, Nagaland, Mizoram, Arunachal Pradesh
Madras HC Tamil Nadu, Puducherry
Punjab & Haryana HC Punjab, Haryana, Chandigarh
Common HC of J&K and Ladakh J&K, Ladakh

3.2 Composition

A Chief Justice and such other judges as the President may from time to time deem necessary (Article 216). Sanctioned strengths vary widely:

  • Allahabad HC — ~160 judges (the largest)
  • Bombay HC — ~94
  • Madras HC — ~75
  • Sikkim HC — ~3 (the smallest)

3.3 Appointment

By the President under Article 217, on the recommendation of the Collegium — CJI + 2 senior-most SC judges, with the HC Collegium (CJ + 2 senior-most HC judges) initiating the proposal for non-Chief Justice posts.

3.4 Writ jurisdiction — Article 226

A High Court's writ jurisdiction is wider than the Supreme Court's Article 32. While Article 32 covers only Fundamental Rights, Article 226 covers writs for any legal right ("for the enforcement of any of the rights conferred by Part III and for any other purpose"). This is why most writ petitions in India are filed in High Courts.

3.5 Supervisory jurisdiction — Article 227

Every High Court has superintendence over all courts and tribunals within its territorial jurisdiction (other than military courts/tribunals).

4 · Subordinate Judiciary (Articles 233–237)

4.1 Appointment

  • District Judges are appointed by the Governor in consultation with the High Court (Article 233).
  • Other judicial officers (Civil Judges, Judicial Magistrates) are appointed under Article 234 by the Governor, in consultation with the State Public Service Commission and the High Court.
  • The High Court controls postings, promotions, leave and discipline (Article 235).

4.2 Two streams

  • Civil: Munsif/Civil Judge (Junior Div.) → Civil Judge (Senior Div.) → Sub-Judge → District Judge → HC.
  • Criminal: Judicial Magistrate Second Class → JM First Class → Chief JM → Sessions Judge → HC.

A unified judicial service has been proposed multiple times (most recently as the All-India Judicial Service) but not implemented.

5 · Tribunals (Articles 323A, 323B)

Part XIV-A was inserted by the 42nd Amendment, 1976. Tribunals are specialised quasi-judicial bodies meant to relieve regular courts and bring subject-matter expertise.

Article What it covers Examples
323A Service matters of public employees (Parliament-only) CAT (Central Administrative Tribunal), established 1985
323B Wider — taxation, FX, labour, land reforms, elections, rent, food (Parliament + states) ITAT, NCLT, NGT, AFT, CESTAT, SAT

5.1 L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997)

A Constitution Bench held that judicial review by High Courts (Art. 226) and the Supreme Court (Art. 32) is part of the Basic Structure of the Constitution. Tribunal decisions are therefore not "final" — they remain subject to writ-jurisdiction scrutiny by the High Courts. Parliament's earlier attempts to oust HC jurisdiction were struck down.

5.2 Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021

Merged several appellate tribunals — including the Film Certification Appellate Tribunal, Airports Appellate Tribunal, IPAB (Intellectual Property Appellate Board) — into existing courts. Standardised tenure and service conditions for tribunal members.

6 · Doctrines and tools the Court actually uses

6.1 Judicial review

The Court's power to strike down any law (Central or State) that violates the Constitution, and any executive action that exceeds legal authority. Derived from the structure of the Constitution and explicitly fortified by Articles 13 (laws inconsistent with FRs), 32, 131, 132, 136, 226.

6.2 Basic Structure

(Covered in detail in Chapter 2.) Kesavananda Bharati (1973) → Minerva Mills (1980) → I.R. Coelho (2007). Parliament can amend, but cannot destroy the basic structure.

6.3 Doctrine of Severability

If a statute is partly unconstitutional, only the offending part is struck down — the rest survives if severable.

6.4 Doctrine of Eclipse

A pre-Constitution law inconsistent with a Fundamental Right is not void; it is in "eclipse" — and revives if the constitutional bar is removed.

6.5 "Continuing Mandamus"

A judicial innovation: the Court doesn't issue a single direction; it retains the case on its docket, requiring periodic reports from the executive on compliance (e.g., the Hawala case, the Vineet Narain prosecution-of-bureaucrats case, NRC Assam).

6.6 Article 142 — "Complete Justice"

The Supreme Court can pass any order it deems necessary "for doing complete justice in any cause or matter pending before it." Used famously in the Bhopal Gas Tragedy settlement, Ayodhya land-allotment direction (2019), and — in 2025 — to deem 10 Tamil Nadu state bills to have received the Governor's assent.

7 · The Court today — three running pressures

  1. Pendency. As of mid-2026, the Supreme Court has roughly 80,000 pending cases, the High Courts collectively have ~60 lakh, and the subordinate judiciary holds ~4.5 crore (45 million). The Court has experimented with constitution benches dedicated to particular question types, virtual hearings post-COVID, and faster certificate-of-fitness routes — but the queue grows.
  2. Judicial appointments. The Collegium is much criticised for opacity, slowness, and "the brother-judge problem." Pending vacancies in HCs hover around 30 %.
  3. Centre-State frictions. The Court has become a more frequent referee between opposition-ruled states and the Union — Governor disputes, ED/CBI actions, Article 356 threats, Article 370 abrogation challenge, electoral bonds.
