Anchored in M. Laxmikanth's Indian Polity and Shivam Shankar Singh's How to Win an Indian Election.

From a Minister's notice on a Monday morning to a statute notified in the Gazette of India, an Indian law travels through a precise constitutional pipeline. Most journeys take months; some take years; a few die in committee; one in a hundred fails at the President's desk. This chapter is the pipeline.

Reading time: ~17 min Pairs with: Laxmikanth, Ch. 22 (Lawmaking sections); PRS Bill Track briefs · Subhash Kashyap, Our Parliament

1 · Four kinds of Bills

Bill type Defined by Where can it be introduced? What's special?
Ordinary Bill Anything not in the other three categories Either House Standard 3-reading process
Money Bill Article 110(1) — strictly fiscal matters Lok Sabha only, with President's prior recommendation Rajya Sabha can only recommend; 14-day window; Speaker certifies finality
Financial Bill Article 117 — covers Money-Bill matters plus other matters; or general matters but with charge on Consolidated Fund Either House (Category I = LS only with Presidential rec) Normal procedure, but needs Presidential recommendation for introduction
Constitutional Amendment Bill Article 368 Either House Special majority each House; some require ratification by ≥ half the states; no joint sitting possible

2.1 First Reading — introduction

The Minister (for a Government Bill) or any other member (for a Private Members' Bill) seeks leave to introduce. Once leave is granted and the Bill is introduced, it is published in the Gazette. There is no debate at this stage. A Bill may also be introduced directly by being published in the Gazette before introduction.

2.2 Reference to a Departmentally Related Standing Committee

After introduction, most Government Bills are referred to the relevant Departmentally Related Standing Committee (DRSC) — one of 24 such committees, each tied to a Ministry or group of Ministries. The Committee may seek public submissions, hear experts, and submit a report with recommended changes. The Committee report is laid before both Houses.

This stage is often where the substantive negotiation happens. For complex bills, a Joint Parliamentary Committee (JPC) or Select Committee of one House may be set up instead.

2.3 Second Reading — discussion and clause-by-clause

Two sub-stages:

  • General discussion. The principles and provisions of the Bill (and the Committee report) are debated. No amendments at this stage.
  • Clause-by-clause consideration. Each clause and schedule is voted on, with amendments offered and decided.

2.4 Third Reading — passing

A brief debate on the Bill as a whole (no further amendments to substance, only verbal). The motion: "That the Bill be passed." A simple majority of members present and voting is enough for an ordinary Bill.

2.5 Transmission to the other House

The same three readings happen there. The other House may:

  • Pass the Bill as received → goes to the President.
  • Pass it with amendments → returns to the originating House; if accepted there, goes to the President; if not, deadlock.
  • Reject it → deadlock.
  • Not take action for 6 months → deadlock.

3 · The joint sitting (Article 108)

When a deadlock occurs on an ordinary or financial bill, the President may summon both Houses to a joint sitting. The matter is decided by simple majority of members present and voting. The combined strength favours the Lok Sabha (currently 543 vs 245), so joint sittings effectively let the lower House's view prevail when needed.

Article 108 cannot be invoked for: - Money Bills — the Lok Sabha's word is final. - Constitutional Amendment Bills — each House must pass separately by special majority.

Only three joint sittings have ever been held: Dowry Prohibition Bill (1961), Banking Service Commission (Repeal) Bill (1978), and Prevention of Terrorism Bill (POTA, 2002).

4 · The Money Bill route (Article 110)

A Money Bill is one that contains only provisions dealing with:

  • (a) imposition, abolition, remission, alteration or regulation of any tax;
  • (b) regulation of the borrowing of money by the Government;
  • (c) custody of the Consolidated Fund or Contingency Fund;
  • (d) appropriation of money out of the Consolidated Fund;
  • (e) declaration of any expenditure as charged on the Consolidated Fund;
  • (f) receipt of money on account of the Consolidated Fund or Public Account;
  • (g) any matter incidental to the above.

The Speaker's certification that a Bill is a Money Bill is final under Article 110(3). The Money Bill route bypasses the Rajya Sabha because:

  1. It can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha on the President's prior recommendation.
  2. Once passed by the Lok Sabha, it is sent to the Rajya Sabha, which can only recommend amendments.
  3. The Rajya Sabha must return the Bill within 14 days with or without recommendations.
  4. The Lok Sabha may accept or reject those recommendations.
  5. If the Rajya Sabha does not act within 14 days, the Bill is deemed passed by both Houses.
The Money Bill controversy The Aadhaar Act was passed as a Money Bill in 2016 even though its substantive provisions extended beyond fiscal matters. The Supreme Court in Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) and Another v. Union of India (2018) upheld the certification, with Justice Chandrachud dissenting that the Speaker's certification was wrong. The five-judge bench in Rojer Mathew v. South Indian Bank Ltd. (2019) referred the question of judicial review of the Speaker's Money Bill certification to a larger bench — pending as of 2026. The route is therefore both an essential federal mechanism and a contested loophole.

5 · The Constitutional Amendment Bill (Article 368)

The most demanding route. Three flavours, three majority thresholds:

Mode Where applies Threshold
Simple majority Provisions outside Article 368 (e.g., Art. 4, 169, parts of 5th/6th Sched.) Simple majority in each House
Special majority Most amendments (FRs, DPSPs, executive/legislative powers within Centre or State, etc.) Majority of total membership of each House AND ≥ 2/3 of members present and voting
Special majority + state ratification Federal-touch provisions: Art. 54–55 (President), Art. 73, 162 (executive), Supreme Court/HC, Schedule VII, representation of states in Parliament, Art. 368 Above + ratification by ≥ ½ of state legislatures

Notes: - No joint sitting is possible — each House must independently pass. - The President must give assent — cannot use suspensive veto (the 24th Amendment of 1971 specifically required the President to assent to constitutional amendments). - The Supreme Court can still strike down a constitutional amendment if it violates the basic structure (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973).

6 · The President's assent (Article 111)

Once a Bill is passed by both Houses (or by joint sitting, or — for a Money Bill — by the Lok Sabha + deemed Rajya Sabha agreement), it goes to the President. Four options:

  1. Assent. The Bill becomes an Act.
  2. Withhold assent. The Bill lapses. This is an "absolute veto" — used very rarely. Examples: Indira Gandhi's withholding assent to a PEPSU Appropriation Bill (1954); a State Government's recommendation lapses when the recommending council of ministers falls.
  3. Return for reconsideration (only for non-Money Bills). This is the "suspensive veto." If Parliament re-passes the Bill — with or without amendments — the President must give assent the second time.
  4. Pocket veto. Neither assent, nor return, nor reject. The Bill simply sits. The Constitution prescribes no time limit, so this is technically possible. The famous instance: President Zail Singh's pocket veto of the Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill, 1986.

For State Bills going to the Governor (Article 200), the choices are broadly the same — assent, return, withhold, or reserve for President's consideration. The Tamil Nadu Governor's prolonged use of pocket-veto-style inaction was halted by the Supreme Court in State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor of Tamil Nadu (April 2025), which "deemed" 10 bills to have received assent under Article 142 (see Chapter 4).

7 · Ordinances (Articles 123, 213)

When Parliament is not in session and the President is satisfied that immediate action is required, the President may promulgate an ordinance under Article 123. An ordinance has the same force as an Act of Parliament. But:

  • It must be laid before both Houses when they reassemble.
  • It ceases to operate six weeks after the date of reassembly unless approved.
  • The President can re-promulgate an ordinance, but the Supreme Court in Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar (2017) held that repeated re-promulgation without seeking parliamentary approval is a "fraud on the Constitution".

Article 213 grants equivalent powers to the Governor at the state level.

9 · Parliamentary Committees — the unsung backbone

Most of the real legislative work happens in committees. Three categories:

9.1 Standing Committees

Financial Committees:

Committee Strength Chair
Public Accounts Committee (PAC) 22 (15 LS + 7 RS) Convention: an Opposition MP
Estimates Committee 30 (LS only) Ruling party / coalition
Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU) 22 (15 LS + 7 RS) —

Departmentally Related Standing Committees (DRSCs): 24 in total, each with 31 members (21 LS + 10 RS), one-year term. Cover all Ministries.

Other Standing Committees: Business Advisory Committee, Rules Committee, Committee on Privileges, General Purposes Committee, Committee on Government Assurances.

9.2 Ad-hoc Committees

Constituted for a specific purpose, then dissolved.

  • Select Committee — of one House.
  • Joint Parliamentary Committee (JPC) — of both Houses.

Famous JPCs: Bofors (1987), 2G spectrum (2011), Land Acquisition Bill (2015), DPDP Bill (2022).

9.3 Why committees matter

Bills referred to DRSCs typically come out substantially amended, with public consultations factored in. Without committee scrutiny, Parliament's role would collapse into "ratify what the executive proposes." With it, Parliament retains some genuine deliberative power — though India's committee-referral rate has dropped sharply over the last decade (~70 % of bills in the 14th–15th LS, ~25 % in the 17th LS, per PRS data).

10 · A worked example — the journey of an ordinary government bill

Let's trace a notional Bill:

  1. Day 1 — Drafting. Ministry of X drafts the Bill. Inter-ministerial consultation. Cabinet approval.
  2. Day 30 — Introduction. Bill listed in the Lok Sabha. Minister introduces; First Reading.
  3. Day 35 — DRSC reference. Bill referred to the relevant Standing Committee.
  4. Days 36–120 — Committee scrutiny. Public submissions invited. Witnesses heard. Clauses debated. Report adopted; tabled in both Houses.
  5. Day 150 — Second Reading in LS. General discussion + clause-by-clause. Government amendments incorporated.
  6. Day 152 — Third Reading. "That the Bill be passed." Voice vote or division.
  7. Day 160 — Rajya Sabha takes up. First Reading → DRSC report referenced → Second Reading → Third Reading.
  8. Day 175 — Bill goes to the President.
  9. Day 195 — President's assent. Becomes an Act of Parliament.
  10. Day 200 — Notification in the Gazette. The Act comes into force on the appointed date (often immediately, sometimes deferred subject to notification of rules under the Act).
