The Constitution describes India as a "Union of States" — never a federation. That phrasing is deliberate. India is federal in structure, with constitutionally protected powers for both Centre and States, but unitary in spirit, with a strong tilt toward the Union. K.C. Wheare called it "quasi-federal." Most scholars today prefer "co-operative federalism" — or, when relations turn rough, "competitive federalism."

Reading time: ~14 min Pairs with: Laxmikanth, Ch. 13–15, 36–37 · Granville Austin (1999), Ch. on Centre-State relations · M.P. Singh, Indian Government and Politics

1 · What "Union of States" means

Article 1 declares: "India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States." Ambedkar, defending the phrasing in the Constituent Assembly, said the Drafting Committee had deliberately avoided "Federation of States" to make three points:

  1. The Indian Union is not the result of an agreement between states (unlike the United States).
  2. States have no right to secede from the Union.
  3. The Union is indestructible, even if individual states are not.

The federal nature was nonetheless affirmed by the Supreme Court in S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994), which held that federalism is part of the basic structure of the Constitution.

Two recent territorial changes

  • 31 October 2019 — The Jammu & Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019, came into force: the former State of Jammu & Kashmir was reorganised into two Union Territories, J&K (with Legislative Assembly) and Ladakh (without). Article 370, which had granted J&K special status, was effectively read down by Presidential Order C.O. 272 on 5 August 2019; the Supreme Court upheld the abrogation in In Re: Article 370 (December 2023) (source).
  • 26 January 2020 — The UTs of Dadra & Nagar Haveli and Daman & Diu were merged into a single Union Territory.

3 · How power is divided — the Seventh Schedule

The Seventh Schedule divides legislative power between the Union and the States across three lists (source):

List Subjects Examples
Union List (List I) ~98 Defence, foreign affairs, atomic energy, railways, banking, currency, post & telegraph, citizenship, war and peace
State List (List II) ~59 Public order, police, local government, public health, agriculture, land, trade & commerce within state, liquor
Concurrent List (List III) ~47 Criminal law and procedure, marriage and divorce, contracts, transfer of property other than agricultural land, education, forests, electricity, economic and social planning

Residuary powers — anything not listed — vest in Parliament under Article 248. Where Union and State laws on a concurrent subject conflict, Union law prevails (Article 254), subject to the safety valve in Art. 254(2) that lets a state law passed with Presidential assent prevail within that state.

When Parliament can legislate on State subjects

Five well-known windows:

  1. Article 249 — In national interest, the Rajya Sabha may pass a resolution by 2/3 majority allowing Parliament to legislate on a State subject for one year (renewable).
  2. Article 250 — During a national emergency (Art. 352), Parliament can legislate on any State subject.
  3. Article 252 — Two or more states may request Parliament to legislate on a State subject for them; other states may adopt that law later.
  4. Article 253 — Parliament can legislate to give effect to international treaties even on State subjects.
  5. Article 356 — During President's Rule, Parliament can legislate on State subjects for that state.

4 · The tilt towards the Centre

K.C. Wheare's "quasi-federal" verdict rests on several design features that load the dice in favour of the Union:

  • Strong Union List with the highest-stakes subjects.
  • Residuary powers with Parliament, not the states.
  • Single Constitution and single citizenship.
  • Integrated judiciary with the Supreme Court at the apex (unlike the US's dual federal/state court systems).
  • All-India Services (IAS, IPS, IFoS) — recruited centrally but cadre-allocated to states.
  • Governor appointed by the President — not by the state's people or legislature.
  • President's Rule under Article 356 — the Centre can suspend an elected state government.
  • Emergency provisions (Articles 352, 356, 360) — temporarily convert federation into a unitary system.
  • Finance Commission (Article 280) — devolution of central taxes is on Centre's recommendations.
  • Parliament's power to alter state boundaries (Article 3) — without state ratification.

5 · Article 356 — President's Rule

Under Article 356, if the President is satisfied that the government of a state cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution, he/she may proclaim that the powers of the state are assumed by the President — known as "President's Rule". Parliament must approve within two months. The state's legislative assembly may be suspended or dissolved.

S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) is the constitutional fence around Article 356:

  • Presidential proclamation under Art. 356 is subject to judicial review.
  • The "majority" of the council of ministers must be tested on the floor of the Assembly, not in the Raj Bhavan.
  • If the Court strikes down the proclamation, the dissolved Assembly can be revived.
  • Secularism is a basic structure feature; dismissal of governments that violate secular norms is therefore proper.

Since Bommai, the use of Art. 356 has dropped dramatically — from a routine political tool in the 1970s–80s to a rarely used emergency lever today.

6 · The Governor question

The Governor, appointed by the President (i.e., on the advice of the Union Council of Ministers) and removable at the President's pleasure, is constitutionally the head of a state but politically often perceived as a Union appointee planted in opposition-ruled states. Three recent Supreme Court interventions tightened the screws on gubernatorial overreach:

  • B.P. Singhal v. Union of India (2010) — The President's pleasure (and hence the power to remove a Governor) is not absolute; it is reviewable on malafides or arbitrariness. A Governor cannot be removed simply because a new party has come to power at the Centre.
  • State of Punjab v. Principal Secretary to the Governor (November 2023) — A bench led by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud held that Governor Banwarilal Purohit could not "doubt the validity of an Assembly session" or sit on bills indefinitely; Governors are symbolic heads who cannot "thwart the normal course of lawmaking" (source).
  • State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor of Tamil Nadu (8 April 2025, 2025 INSC 481) — A Division Bench of Justices J.B. Pardiwala and R. Mahadevan unanimously held that Tamil Nadu Governor R.N. Ravi's withholding of 10 state bills — some pending since January 2020 — was "illegal and erroneous". Article 200, the Court held, does not give the Governor an absolute or pocket veto; once a Bill is re-passed after reconsideration, the Governor is bound to give assent. The Court fixed indicative timelines: one month with ministerial advice; three months without; one month for re-passed bills. Invoking Article 142, the Court — for the first time in Indian constitutional history — "deemed" 10 state bills to have received the Governor's assent (source).
Why this matters The Tamil Nadu judgment effectively closes the "pocket veto" loophole at the state level. Combined with Singhal, it converts the Governor's job — for the first time clearly — into a constitutionally constrained role rather than a discretionary political post.

7 · Delhi — a special case

Delhi is not just any UT. Article 239AA (inserted by the 69th Amendment, 1991) gives Delhi a Legislative Assembly with limited subject-matter jurisdiction — it cannot legislate on public order, police, or land. Two recent constitutional moments redefined the balance:

  • Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India (11 May 2023) — A five-judge Constitution Bench unanimously held that the elected Delhi government has legislative and executive power over "services" (Entry 41, List II), except for public order, police and land. The Court built a "triple chain of accountability": civil servants → ministers → assembly → electorate (source).
  • GNCTD (Amendment) Act, 2023 (effective 11 August 2023) — Parliament effectively overrode the verdict, creating the National Capital Civil Services Authority (NCCSA) — a three-member body chaired by the Delhi CM but with the Chief Secretary and Principal Home Secretary as the other members, and with the Lieutenant Governor having the final say on its recommendations (source).

8 · Three flavours of Indian federalism

  • Co-operative federalism — the model promoted by the NITI Aayog, GST Council, and inter-state councils. Centre and states pool resources and ideas. GST is the canonical example: states surrendered their power to tax sale of goods in return for guaranteed compensation and a joint decision-making body.
  • Competitive federalism — states compete on rankings (Ease of Doing Business, school attendance, vaccination coverage) for central grants and private investment.
  • Confrontational federalism — opposition-ruled states vs Centre. Recent flashpoints: Governor's withholding assent (TN, Kerala, Punjab); central agency action in opposition states (ED, CBI, IT); delimitation politics; GST compensation disputes; cross-border issues like cattle smuggling and language imposition.

9 · The Finance Commission and tax devolution

Tax devolution — what share of central tax revenue goes to the states — is decided every five years by a Finance Commission constituted under Article 280. Headline numbers:

  • 14th FC (Y.V. Reddy, 2015–20) — raised states' share of the divisible pool from 32 % to 42 %.
  • 15th FC (N.K. Singh, 2021–26) — set the share at 41 % (down by one percentage point because J&K and Ladakh became UTs in 2019).
  • 16th FC (Arvind Panagariya, 2026–31) — report tabled in Parliament on 1 February 2026; retained the 41 % share for states (source).

Horizontal allocation among states is decided by formula — historically a mix of population, income distance, area, demographic performance, forest cover and tax effort.
