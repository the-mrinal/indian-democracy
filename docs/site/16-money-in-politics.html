Anchored in M. Laxmikanth's Indian Polity and Shivam Shankar Singh's How to Win an Indian Election.

The 2024 Lok Sabha election cost an estimated ₹1 lakh crore — making it, by some accounts, the most expensive election in world history. The legal cap on each Lok Sabha candidate's spending is ₹95 lakh. The gap between those two numbers is where the story of Indian political finance lives — and where the Supreme Court's 15 February 2024 judgment striking down the Electoral Bonds scheme set a new constitutional fence.

Reading time: ~17 min Pairs with: Milan Vaishnav, When Crime Pays · ADR reports · ECI annual contribution reports · PRS briefs on Electoral Bonds

2 · The candidate-expenditure regime

2.1 The 2022 cap

In January 2022, the ECI revised candidate expenditure ceilings (source):

Office Larger states Smaller states
Lok Sabha ₹95 lakh ₹75 lakh
Assembly ₹40 lakh ₹28 lakh

Earlier ceilings (2014): ₹70 lakh / ₹54 lakh / ₹28 lakh / ₹20 lakh.

2.2 What counts and what doesn't

Counts towards the cap: - Public meetings, rallies, processions - Posters, banners, pamphlets, hoardings - Loudspeakers, vehicles, fuel - Campaign office rent, staff payments - Personal travel of the candidate (and 50 % of star-campaigner travel if travelling together)

Does not count: - Star campaigners' travel (under Section 77, RPA) — unless the candidate is co-travelling or sharing the dais. - Spending by the political party itself — no statutory cap.

2.3 Filing the expense return

Each candidate must file a statement of election expenses within 30 days of result declaration to the District Election Officer. Failure to file or non-compliance can result in disqualification for 3 years under Section 10A of the RPA, 1951. The expenses statement is published on the ECI website.

2.4 The reality gap

The candidate cap has long been a fiction. Practitioners — including Shivam Shankar Singh in How to Win an Indian Election — estimate that actual per-seat spending in a competitive Lok Sabha contest routinely runs into several crores, with high-profile seats reportedly costing tens of crores.

The Centre for Media Studies (CMS) estimate for 2024 Lok Sabha: ~₹1,00,000 crore across all parties and candidates, with some experts pegging the total as high as ₹1.35 lakh crore (source).

3 · How parties get money

3.1 Membership and voluntary donations

Membership fees are nominal. Voluntary contributions from individuals are common but rarely large.

3.2 Corporate donations

Under Section 182 of the Companies Act, 2013, a corporate donor must:

  • Disclose the political party-wise breakdown in its annual report;
  • Pay through a banking channel.

The 7.5 % cap — donations could not exceed 7.5 % of the company's average net profit over the previous three years — was removed by an amendment in the Finance Act, 2017 (alongside the introduction of Electoral Bonds). The amendment was struck down by the Supreme Court in February 2024, restoring the cap and disclosure regime.

3.3 Electoral Trusts

The Electoral Trusts Scheme, 2013 allows companies to contribute to a registered Electoral Trust, which in turn distributes to political parties. The Trust must distribute at least 95 % of its receipts in the financial year to parties registered under Section 29A. Donations are tax-exempt under Section 13B of the IT Act. After the death of Electoral Bonds, Electoral Trusts have become a primary channel.

3.4 Cash — the unaccounted reality

The candidate-cap loophole is enabled by the simple fact that a vast portion of political spending is in cash and unaccounted. Election-time cash seizures by the ECI's flying squads in 2024 ran into thousands of crores. The "envelope politics" of distributing cash, liquor, sarees, and gifts in the last 48 hours is openly discussed in practitioner accounts (Singh, Vaishnav).

4 · The Electoral Bonds saga (2018 → 2024)

4.1 What was it?

The Electoral Bond Scheme, announced by FM Arun Jaitley in the 2017–18 Budget and notified on 2 January 2018:

  • A donor could buy interest-free bearer bonds from the State Bank of India in denominations of ₹1,000 / ₹10,000 / ₹1 lakh / ₹10 lakh / ₹1 crore.
  • The bonds could be deposited with any party registered under Section 29A that had secured at least 1 % of votes in the most recent LS/Assembly election.
  • The party could encash the bond within 15 days of issue.
  • Donor identity was hidden from the recipient party, the ECI, and the public.
  • The 2018 scheme also removed the 7.5 % cap on corporate donations and the disclosure requirement under Section 182 of the Companies Act.

4.2 What was wrong with it?

The opposition critique, accepted by the SC, was:

  1. Voter's right to know — under Article 19(1)(a) — extends to knowing who funds parties. Anonymous bonds destroyed that right.
  2. Removal of the 7.5 % cap allowed loss-making and shell companies to donate unlimited sums, opening the door to laundering.
  3. Disproportionate flow to the ruling party — over 50 % of bond receipts went to the BJP.
  4. The SBI bottleneck — even if the donor's identity was hidden from the public, the government knew (via SBI) who was funding whom, creating a structural information asymmetry.

4.3 The judgment — 15 February 2024

A 5-judge SC Constitution Bench led by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud (with Justices Sanjiv Khanna, B.R. Gavai, J.B. Pardiwala and Manoj Misra) unanimously struck down:

  • The Electoral Bond Scheme itself as violative of Article 19(1)(a) (source).
  • The Companies Act amendment removing the 7.5 % cap (restoring the pre-2017 regime).
  • The Income-tax Act amendment that allowed non-disclosure of electoral bond donors.

The Court directed SBI to disclose to the ECI all bond purchases from 12 April 2019 onwards. On 11 March 2024, the Court ordered SBI to hand over all data by next-day close of business; the ECI published the data on 15 March 2024.

4.4 What the data revealed

  • BJP was the largest beneficiary, receiving approximately 47 % of total bond value (~₹6,000 crore).
  • Other major recipients: TMC, INC, BRS, BJD, DMK, AAP, AIADMK, JD(S).
  • Top corporate donors (revealed): Megha Engineering & Infrastructure (MEIL), Quick Supply Chain Pvt Ltd (linked to the Adani group), Vedanta, Bharti Airtel, Grasim, Aurobindo Pharma, Reliance, and others.
  • Investigative journalism revealed several donors with overlapping enforcement-action histories — raising the "quid pro quo" critique.

4.5 What replaced the bonds?

Nothing legislated. The election ecosystem has reverted to:

  • Electoral Trusts (Prudent, Triumph, etc.).
  • Direct corporate donations subject to the 7.5 % cap and disclosure.
  • Cash and other unaccounted means.

A successor transparency framework has been the subject of multiple PRS and ADR proposals (e.g., a "National Election Fund" with state matching grants and per-vote subsidies), but none has been legislated.

5 · Criminalisation of politics — Vaishnav's When Crime Pays

5.1 The book's central question

Milan Vaishnav's When Crime Pays: Money and Muscle in Indian Politics (Yale University Press / HarperCollins India, 2017) asks: why do parties keep picking candidates with serious criminal cases, and why do voters keep electing them? (source)

5.2 The "demand-side" theory

Vaishnav's answer reverses the usual moral framing. In contexts of:

  • Weak state delivery (police don't show up, schools don't function, courts take decades);
  • Fragmented multi-party contests (no candidate has a majority; co-ethnic mobilisation matters);
  • Caste/community arithmetic dominating candidate selection;

— a candidate with a reputation for delivering goods, dispensing rough justice, and protecting community interests has a real electoral advantage. A criminal record can signal exactly this capacity.

Parties pick such candidates because they are:

  • Self-financing — they bring their own money, reducing demand on the party kitty.
  • Winnable — empirically, ADR data shows candidates with criminal cases have higher winnability than clean candidates.

5.3 ADR data — the 2024 Lok Sabha

The Association for Democratic Reforms (ADR) has tracked the criminal antecedents of every MP and MLA in India since 2003 via the affidavits candidates must file with the ECI under SC directions in Union of India v. ADR (2002, 2003).

The headline numbers for the 18th Lok Sabha (elected June 2024) (source):

Metric Number
MPs with declared criminal cases 251 / 543 (46 %) — record high
MPs with serious criminal cases (rape, murder, attempt to murder, kidnapping, crimes against women) 170 / 543 (31 %)
MPs already convicted 27
Crorepatis 504 / 543 (93 %)
Women MPs 74 / 543 (14 %)

Increase since 2009: +55 % in MPs with criminal cases; +124 % in MPs with serious cases.

Party-wise breakdown of serious cases (top three):

Party MPs With serious cases %
BJP 240 63 26 %
INC 99 32 32 %
SP 37 17 46 %

ADR separately found that candidates with criminal cases in 2024 had significantly higher winnability than clean candidates (source).

5.4 What the Supreme Court has tried

The SC has tried several routes:

  • Union of India v. ADR (2002, 2003) — Compulsory disclosure by candidates of criminal record, assets and liabilities, education.
  • Lily Thomas v. UoI (2013) — Struck down Section 8(4) of the RPA. A sitting MP/MLA convicted of an offence carrying ≥ 2 years' imprisonment is disqualified immediately (no 3-month appeal window).
  • Public Interest Foundation v. UoI (2018) — Parties must publish criminal antecedents of candidates on their websites, in newspapers, and on social media.
  • Brajesh Singh v. Sunil Arora (2021, 2022) — Strengthened the 2018 directions; non-compliance = contempt.

None of these has substantially shifted candidate-selection behaviour at the party level. Vaishnav's logic — winnability beats disclosure — continues to hold.

7 · Three reform proposals on the table

  1. State funding of elections — periodically proposed (Indrajit Gupta Committee 1998; Law Commission 2015; ADR-Vaishnav proposals). Idea: per-vote subsidy from the public exchequer, conditional on transparency. None enacted.
  2. Cap on party spending — closing the central loophole. Resisted by all major parties.
  3. National Election Fund — companies could donate to the Fund instead of to parties; the Fund would distribute based on prior vote share. Proposed by the Election Commission itself in 2004 and 2016. Not adopted.
