Anchored in M. Laxmikanth's Indian Polity and Shivam Shankar Singh's How to Win an Indian Election.

Everything in the previous sixteen chapters is the system architecture. This chapter is the execution layer — how a party identifies, persuades, mobilises and turns out voters, booth by booth, in the real campaign. The single best source for this is Shivam Shankar Singh's How to Win an Indian Election: What Political Parties Don't Want You to Know (Penguin, 2019), written from inside the BJP's data and field operations.

Reading time: ~22 min Pairs with: Shivam Shankar Singh, How to Win an Indian Election (Penguin, 2019) · Singh & Venkatanarayanan, The Art of Conjuring Alternate Realities (HarperCollins, 2021) · Prashant Kishor / IPAC public interviews · Milan Vaishnav, When Crime Pays

1 · The book that decoded the campaign

Shivam Shankar Singh was an IT professional who joined the BJP's data analytics team and led campaign data work for the Manipur Assembly Election (2017) and the Tripura Assembly Election (2018) — both of which delivered surprise BJP wins. He later worked briefly with Prashant Kishor's I-PAC in Punjab.

In 2019, he published How to Win an Indian Election: What Political Parties Don't Want You to Know (Penguin Random House India, ISBN 978-0143446842). It is the closest thing to an honest operating manual for an Indian campaign — written by someone who has built one, and willing to name names and numbers.

His follow-up book, The Art of Conjuring Alternate Realities: How Information Warfare Shapes Your World (HarperCollins India, 2021, with Anand Venkatanarayanan), pivots to disinformation, IT cells, reflexive control, and platform-level information warfare (source).

2 · The smallest unit: the polling booth

Indian electoral organisation is built booth by booth.

2.1 BLO vs BLA — two parallel networks

Role Who appoints? Job
Booth Level Officer (BLO) Election Commission of India Maintains the electoral roll for that booth — adds, deletes, corrects entries; visits door to door for verification
Booth Level Agent (BLA) The political party Accompanies the BLO on enumeration; witnesses additions/deletions; on poll day acts as the party's polling agent

The BLO is typically a government employee — a school teacher, an Anganwadi worker, a panchayat secretary, a village-level worker. The BLA is a party karyakarta — usually the most engaged local cadre.

Singh's key point: a party that controls its BLA network controls the electoral roll at the most granular level. Adding genuine supporters and challenging suspected opposition supporters is the most cost-effective vote-multiplier in Indian elections.

2.2 The Panna Pramukh model

A BJP innovation popularised by Singh's book:

  • A single double-sided page of the electoral roll holds around 60 names.
  • For each page, a panna pramukh ("page chief") karyakarta is assigned.
  • The panna pramukh is personally responsible for those ~60 voters — from voter-list verification, to issue understanding, to slip distribution on poll day, to turnout mobilisation.

The model translates abstract organisational strength into accountable, voter-level coverage. It is widely credited as a central pillar of BJP electoral dominance from 2014 onwards. Similar models are now used by TMC ("Banglar Garbo Mamata"), DMK's volunteer cadre system, and YSRCP/TVK.

2.3 Booth classification — friendly, hostile, swing

Internal party data classifies each booth into one of three categories:

  • Friendly (booth where the party historically wins by margin): focus on GOTV — get out the vote — provide transport for the elderly, distribute parchis, deploy panna pramukhs intensively.
  • Hostile (party historically loses by margin): focus on suppression / contestation — file objections to suspected dummy voters, challenge polling-process irregularities, ensure at least token presence.
  • Swing (party wins/loses by < 5 %): focus on saturation messaging — door-to-door visits, repeated cadre touches, targeted local welfare announcements.

Resource allocation across the campaign — money, cadre, candidate visits, social media — follows this classification.

2.4 Parchi vitran — slip distribution

Just before polling day, the BLA network distributes a parchi (slip) to every voter on the page, listing their booth number, serial number on the voter list, and section. This dramatically lowers the friction of voting — the voter knows exactly where to go and what number to quote. Parties without an effective BLA network rely on the ECI's official slip distribution, which is typically less timely and less complete.

3 · Voter data — surveys, microtargeting, beneficiary lists

3.1 Surveys

A serious campaign commissions three layers of survey research:

  1. Baseline (pre-campaign) — typically T-90 to T-60 days. Maps the electorate by caste/community, key issues, name recognition, anti-incumbency, candidate evaluation.
  2. Tracking surveys — every 2–4 weeks during the campaign. Tracks shifting preferences; flags new issues; tests messaging.
  3. Internal exit polls — booth-level, on poll day. Drives last-minute GOTV in the hours before poll-close.

Most major campaigns now use a combination of in-house teams, professional firms (CVoter, Today's Chanakya, Lokniti-CSDS, Axis My India, etc.), and bespoke pollsters (Prashant Kishor's I-PAC, Sunil Kanugolu's various firms).

3.2 Microtargeting

Microtargeting uses voter rolls merged with:

  • Mobile numbers — tied to voter IDs via panchayat-level enumeration or commercial datasets.
  • DBT beneficiary lists — PMAY, Ujjwala, PMJDY, PM-KISAN, the new Ladli Behna scheme (MP/Maharashtra), Mukhyamantri Mahila Samman (Delhi), and various state-level cash transfer programmes.
  • Census tract data — caste/community demographics by booth.

A single voter file can carry dozens of attributes — caste, religion, age, mobile, beneficiary status of N schemes — enabling highly targeted messaging. The same voter receives different WhatsApp messages on different issues depending on what the party thinks will move them.

3.3 Scheme branding as a campaign tool

Singh's analysis is blunt: incumbency advantage in India comes substantially through government-scheme branding.

  • PMAY (housing) — ~4 crore beneficiaries; each gets visible BJP-branded foundation-laying and possession ceremonies.
  • PMJDY (Jan Dhan accounts) — ~50 crore accounts; SMS messages on direct benefit transfers.
  • Ujjwala (LPG) — ~10 crore connections to women in BPL households.
  • PM-KISAN — ₹6,000/year DBT to farmers; ~10 crore beneficiaries.
  • Ladli Behna (MP, 2023; Maharashtra, 2024) — monthly DBT to women, widely credited with sealing BJP wins in both states.

The challenger's counter-strategy: the "victim card" — framing one's caste or community as historically excluded by the incumbent regime, paired with promises of expanded entitlements (caste census, expanded reservation, new cash transfers).

4 · The IT cell, WhatsApp, and social media

4.1 What an IT cell does

A modern IT cell is a hybrid of:

  • Content production: short videos, memes, infographics, "morning forwards."
  • WhatsApp group management: thousands of small group chats organised by booth, locality, profession, language, caste.
  • Facebook / Instagram / X / YouTube campaigns: paid promotion + organic posts.
  • Defensive operations: monitoring opposition narratives, manufacturing counter-narratives.
  • Offensive operations: targeting opposition leaders, "troll armies", coordinated hashtag campaigns.

4.2 The disinformation problem

Singh and Venkatanarayanan's The Art of Conjuring Alternate Realities (2021) dissects how:

  • Reflexive control is used — feeding the opposition's information ecosystem a desired counter-narrative.
  • Deepfakes and AI-generated content have made forensic verification harder.
  • Platform incentives (engagement-maximising algorithms) reward outrage and division.

The 2024 LS election saw a dramatic rise in AI-generated audio and video deepfakes, including impersonations of major leaders. The ECI's response — an MCC update on deepfakes and a fast-track grievance redressal — has been only partially effective.

4.3 Cambridge Analytica — the India footprint

In 2018, the Cambridge Analytica scandal touched India when Ovleno Business Intelligence (OBI) — CA's India partner — listed the BJP, the Congress, and the JD(U) among its clients. OBI's Managing Director Amrish Tyagi was the son of senior JD(U) leader K.C. Tyagi. SCL India was a joint venture between OBI and the SCL Group, London (source).

The episode underlined how psychographic profiling, A/B-tested messaging and behavioural targeting had moved from American campaigns to Indian ones.

5 · The campaign budget — where the money actually goes

The official Lok Sabha candidate cap is ₹95 lakh; actual spending on a competitive seat routinely runs into several crores. Singh's book breaks down the typical line items:

Line item Approximate share
Cash to booth-level workers / panna pramukhs 20–30 %
Vehicles, fuel, drivers, food 10–15 %
Rallies — staging, sound, crowd logistics, hospitality 10–15 %
Posters, hoardings, flexes, pamphlets 5–10 %
Social media + IT cell salaries 5–10 %
Surveys + internal polling 2–5 %
Helicopter / charter logistics for star campaigners 5–10 %
"Envelope politics" — cash, liquor, gifts on poll-eve 10–20 %
Legal, contingency, miscellaneous 5–10 %

The numbers are illustrative — they vary wildly by state, by seat competitiveness, by candidate's personal style. What is consistent: the candidate's official ₹95 lakh return bears little relation to actual expenditure.

6 · Caste, community, and the math of selection

Most Indian constituencies are won not by majorities of one caste but by coalitions of communities — typically 2 or 3 large blocs combined.

The campaign's first job is enumeration: how many voters of each caste/community at each booth. The next job is issue-mapping: what does each bloc care about, and what credible promise can the candidate make to it. The third job is mobiliser identification: in each community, who can be trusted to deliver votes for cash or for the prospect of patronage.

Singh's book is candid about the role of "vote contractors" and local strongmen who agree to deliver a fixed bloc of votes for fixed consideration — money, future contracts, or post-election patronage. This is a known feature of the electoral landscape, not a deviation from it.

7 · Poll day — the final 24 hours

The final 24 hours are highly choreographed:

  • 6 a.m. — All booth karyakartas activated. Personal calls to the most committed voters.
  • 7 a.m. – 12 noon — High-yield window. Friendly voters are encouraged to vote early ("get it done before lunch"). Transport is provided where lawful (and sometimes where not).
  • 12 noon – 4 p.m. — Hot lull. Persuadable swing voters are called/visited. Door-to-door reminders.
  • 4 p.m. – 6 p.m. — Final push. Panna pramukhs cross-check their pages; chase the gap; deploy backup transport.
  • 6 p.m. – poll close — Last-mile mobilisation. Polling agents alert party HQ to suspected irregularities.

After poll close:

  • EVMs sealed in the presence of polling agents.
  • EVMs transported under armed escort to strongrooms at the counting centre.
  • Counting agents at each table on counting day.

8 · Post-election: thank-you and reward

A campaign doesn't end on poll day. The post-result phase is structured:

  • Thank-you tours — winning candidates and party leaders tour constituencies thanking voters and karyakartas.
  • Rewards for loyal cadres — paid roles in MP/MLA offices, party positions, contracts, government appointments.
  • Punishments for defectors / non-performers — withdrawal of patronage, reassignment to less-favoured booths next time.

The implicit contract — vote for us, and we'll deliver patronage to those who delivered the vote — is central to how parties maintain their booth-level networks between elections.

9 · How this connects to everything else in the book

Reading Singh in parallel with Laxmikanth gives you both halves of Indian democracy:

Laxmikanth says Singh adds
MPs are elected via FPTP Won on the back of booth-by-booth panna pramukh networks
The MCC restricts campaign behaviour But cash distribution, gifts and "envelope politics" persist
Candidate spending is capped at ₹95 lakh Actual spending often 5–50× that
ECI's BLO maintains the roll Parties' BLAs shape it
The Constitution guarantees free and fair elections Whether the election is "free and fair" depends on which side's IT cell ran a more effective WhatsApp campaign

Neither account is complete without the other. The Constitution gives you the rules. The ground execution shows you how the game is actually played within (and sometimes around) those rules.
