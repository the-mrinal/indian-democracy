The Lok Sabha is the directly-elected lower house of Parliament — 543 MPs chosen by ~969 million voters across 543 constituencies using First-Past-The-Post. Constitutionally, it is the chamber to which the Government is answerable; politically, it is where Indian democracy's loudest contests are decided.

Reading time: ~22 min Pairs with: Laxmikanth, Ch. 22–23 · Subhash Kashyap, Our Parliament · PRS Legislative Research briefs

1 · What the Lok Sabha is

The Constitution names it the "House of the People" (Article 79: "There shall be a Parliament for the Union which shall consist of the President and two Houses to be known respectively as the Council of States and the House of the People."). In Hindi, "Lok Sabha" means literally "the assembly of people."

It is the first chamber for ordinary legislation, the exclusive originator of Money Bills, and the only chamber to which the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible. A government that loses a no-confidence motion in the Lok Sabha must resign — the Rajya Sabha has no such power.

2 · Strength and reservation

Maximum strength under Article 81: 550, comprising: - Not more than 530 members representing the States; - Not more than 20 members representing the Union Territories.

Until 2020, the President could also nominate two Anglo-Indians under Article 331. That provision was abolished by the 104th Amendment, effective 25 January 2020 — so the House strength fell from 545 to 543 elected members (source).

Reserved seats under Articles 330 and 332:

Category Seats reserved
Scheduled Castes (SC) 84
Scheduled Tribes (ST) 47
General (unreserved) 412

SC/ST reservation was extended until 25 January 2030 by the 104th Amendment (source). The 106th Amendment, 2023 adds one-third reservation for women on top of the existing SC/ST scheme — but only kicks in after the next delimitation following the first post-2026 census.

3 · How members are elected

  • System: First-Past-The-Post (FPTP) in single-member territorial constituencies.
  • Franchise: Universal adult suffrage (Article 326). Voting age was lowered from 21 to 18 by the 61st Amendment, 1988 (effective 28 March 1989) — at one stroke adding roughly 5 crore (50 million) new voters.
  • Eligibility (to contest): Citizen of India, at least 25 years old, name on an electoral roll, not disqualified under the RPA 1951 (e.g., undischarged insolvent, convicted of a serious offence, holds an office of profit, etc.).
  • Term: 5 years from the date of the first meeting; can be dissolved earlier by the President on the PM's advice. The term can be extended for one year at a time during a Proclamation of Emergency under Article 352.
  • Reserved seats: Only members of SC/ST communities can contest from SC/ST-reserved constituencies — but all voters in the constituency vote, irrespective of caste.

4 · Seat allocation across States and UTs (June 2026)

Seats are distributed roughly in proportion to 1971 census population — frozen by the 42nd Amendment, 1976 and extended by the 84th Amendment, 2001 until "the first census after 2026." The 87th Amendment, 2003 allowed intra-state boundary changes on the 2001 census without altering each state's total.

5 · Delimitation — and the 2026 fight

A Delimitation Commission redraws constituency boundaries after each census. India has had four — 1952, 1962, 1972, and 2002. Each year of constitution, the Commission is set up by an Act of Parliament; its orders cannot be questioned in any court (Article 329).

The post-2026 delimitation is the most politically explosive constitutional moment on the horizon. The state-wise allocation has been frozen on 1971 census numbers since 1976. If reapportioned on 2027 census numbers, the calculation looks roughly like this:

  • Northern states with high fertility (UP, Bihar, Rajasthan, MP) — likely gain seats.
  • Southern states with low fertility (Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Karnataka, Andhra) — likely lose share even if total Lok Sabha size goes up.

On 16 April 2026, the Union Government tabled three Bills in the Lok Sabha — the Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026; the Union Territories Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026; and the Delimitation Bill, 2026 — proposing to enlarge the Lok Sabha and run the delimitation. The Constitution (131st) Bill failed by 298–230 — well short of the 352 needed for the special majority (source). The pre-2026 status quo on seat allocation therefore continues at the time of writing. The fight will return — and women's reservation under the 106th Amendment is hostage to this same delimitation.

Why this matters India's federation rests, in part, on the assumption that each state's voice in Parliament is roughly proportional to its population at some reasonable census date. A purely "fair" redistribution on 2027 numbers would reward states that had less effective family-planning programmes and punish those that succeeded. Reconciling federal arithmetic with demographic reality is the next decade's hardest constitutional problem.

6 · The Speaker and Deputy Speaker

The Speaker is the presiding officer of the Lok Sabha — and one of the most powerful constitutional offices in the country.

6.1 Election and removal

  • Elected by Lok Sabha members from amongst themselves on a date fixed by the President (Articles 93–94).
  • Term coincides with the life of the Lok Sabha.
  • Removal: by a resolution passed by an effective majority (majority of all then members) of the Lok Sabha, after 14 days' notice (Article 94) (source).
  • During discussion of a removal motion, the Speaker does not preside (Article 96) but may speak and vote in the first instance.

6.2 Powers

  • Presides over sittings; maintains order.
  • Casts a vote only in case of a tie (Article 100(1)) — i.e., no "first vote."
  • Decides whether a Bill is a Money Bill (Article 110(3)) — and that decision is final.
  • Certifies authoritative texts of Bills passed by the Lok Sabha.
  • Presides over joint sittings of both Houses (Article 108).
  • Decides on disqualification under the Tenth Schedule (anti-defection); decision subject to judicial review per Kihoto Hollohan.

6.3 Current Speaker

Om Birla (BJP), MP from Kota (Rajasthan), was re-elected Speaker on 26 June 2024. He is only the second Speaker in Lok Sabha history to be elected for two consecutive terms — after Balram Jakhar (1980–1989) (source).

7 · The Leader of the Opposition

The Leader of the Opposition (LOP) in the Lok Sabha is recognised under the Salary and Allowances of Leaders of Opposition in Parliament Act, 1977. The leader of the largest opposition party in the House is granted LOP status only if that party has at least 10 % of the total strength (i.e., 55 seats) — a convention that traces back to a 1956 ruling by Speaker M.A. Ayyangar.

In 2014 (16th LS) and 2019 (17th LS), no opposition party crossed the 55-seat threshold — the Congress had 44 and 52 seats respectively — so the post remained vacant.

In 2024, the Congress won 99 seats and Rahul Gandhi was sworn in as Leader of the Opposition in June 2024 — the first formally recognised LOP since 2014.

8 · Three concepts you cannot avoid

8.1 Quorum (Article 100)

The quorum to conduct business is one-tenth of the total membership of the House — currently 55 members. If quorum is not present, the Speaker must adjourn or suspend the sitting.

8.2 Whips and party discipline

A whip is a written direction issued by a party to its members to vote a particular way (or to be present). Three intensities: - One-line whip — to be present. - Two-line whip — to be present and vote along party lines. - Three-line whip — strictest; defying it triggers anti-defection (Tenth Schedule) consequences.

8.3 No-confidence motion

A motion of no-confidence in the Council of Ministers can be moved only in the Lok Sabha. The Speaker may admit it if at least 50 members support the motion. If carried, the Council of Ministers must resign. Famous instances: Morarji Desai (1979), V.P. Singh (1990), H.D. Deve Gowda (1997), I.K. Gujral (1997 — withdrew before vote), Atal Bihari Vajpayee (1999 — lost by 1 vote). The 2018 no-confidence motion against Modi-I was defeated 325–126.

9.2 Office-bearers

  • Speaker — Om Birla (BJP).
  • Leader of Opposition — Rahul Gandhi (INC).
  • Parliamentary Affairs Minister — Kiren Rijiju.

9.3 What changed in 2024

  • BJP lost its standalone majority for the first time since 2014.
  • The Modi government became coalition-dependent on TDP (16) and JD(U) (12).
  • The INDIA bloc made sharp gains in Uttar Pradesh (SP 37, BJP only 33), Maharashtra (Mahayuti collapse after the BJP-engineered Shiv Sena and NCP splits backfired electorally), and Tamil Nadu (DMK-led INDIA sweep).
  • A formally recognised Leader of Opposition returned to Parliament for the first time in a decade.
