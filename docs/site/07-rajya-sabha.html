The Rajya Sabha is Parliament's "permanent" upper house — 245 members, never dissolved, one-third retiring every two years. Its members are not elected by you and me. They are chosen indirectly by the elected MLAs of state assemblies through a Single Transferable Vote system. The Rajya Sabha exists for one core reason: to give the states, not the people, a voice in the Union legislature.

Reading time: ~16 min Pairs with: Laxmikanth, Ch. 22 · Subhash Kashyap, Our Parliament

1 · What the Rajya Sabha is

Article 80 establishes the Council of States (Rajya Sabha) as the second chamber of Parliament. It is:

  • Indirectly elected (mostly) by MLAs of the State Legislative Assemblies, with 12 members nominated by the President.
  • A permanent body — it is never dissolved. One-third of its members retire every two years, and fresh elections fill those seats.
  • The chamber that represents States and Union Territories rather than the directly aggregated population — the federal counterweight to the popular Lok Sabha.

2 · Strength

Maximum strength under Article 80: 250, comprising:

  • Up to 238 elected representatives of the States and the Union Territories of Delhi, Puducherry, and Jammu & Kashmir;
  • 12 members nominated by the President for distinguished service in literature, science, art, and social service (Article 80(1)(a) and (3)) — a provision borrowed from the Irish Constitution.

Current actual strength: 245 (233 elected + 12 nominated).

The remaining UTs — Andaman & Nicobar, Chandigarh, Dadra & Nagar Haveli + Daman & Diu, Ladakh, Lakshadweep — do not send representatives to the Rajya Sabha.

3 · Term and rotation

  • Each member serves a 6-year term (Article 83).
  • One-third of the members retire every two years. This means there is always continuity in the chamber and no single election can flip its composition.
  • The Rajya Sabha cannot be dissolved (compare with the Lok Sabha, which the President can dissolve at any time).

4 · How members are elected — indirect, STV-PR

The Constitution prescribes that elected members from each State are chosen by the elected members of the Legislative Assembly of that State through a Single Transferable Vote system of Proportional Representation.

4.1 The math, simply

Suppose a State has 15 seats to fill in the RS election and its Assembly has 300 elected MLAs. The "quota" — the minimum votes a candidate needs to win — is calculated using the Droop quota formula:

Quota = (Total valid votes ÷ (Seats + 1)) + 1

For 15 seats and 300 MLAs voting: Quota = (300 ÷ 16) + 1 = 19.75 ≈ 20.

So each candidate needs ~20 first-preference votes to be elected. Surplus votes (anything above the quota) are transferred to next-preference candidates with a calculated transfer value. Candidates with the fewest votes are eliminated and their votes transferred. The process continues until all 15 seats are filled.

4.2 Open ballot since 2003

Voting was originally by secret ballot. The Representation of the People (Amendment) Act, 2003 made it an open ballot: an MLA's preferences are shown to the authorised representative of his/her party. This was a direct response to cross-voting — and was upheld in Kuldip Nayar v. Union of India (2006).

4.3 Why no domicile requirement now

The 2003 amendment also removed the requirement that a Rajya Sabha candidate be ordinarily resident in the state from which he/she is being elected. This is why, for example, a person from Gujarat can contest from Karnataka, or a person from Delhi can contest from Madhya Pradesh.

5 · The Chairperson and Deputy Chairperson

  • The Vice-President of India is the ex officio Chairperson of the Rajya Sabha (Article 64). He/she is not a member of the Rajya Sabha and casts a vote only in case of a tie.
  • The Deputy Chairperson is elected from amongst the members by the Rajya Sabha. Term coincides with membership; removal by an effective majority + 14 days' notice.

The current Chairperson is Vice-President C.P. Radhakrishnan (15th VP, sworn in 12 September 2025 after the resignation of Jagdeep Dhankhar).

7 · Exclusive powers of the Rajya Sabha

The Rajya Sabha has two unique constitutional powers the Lok Sabha cannot exercise:

7.1 Article 249 — Legislating on a State subject

If the Rajya Sabha passes a resolution by a 2/3 majority of members present and voting declaring that "it is necessary or expedient in the national interest" that Parliament should make laws on a matter in the State List, Parliament can do so for up to one year. This is renewable.

7.2 Article 312 — Creating a new All-India Service

Parliament can create a new All-India Service (additional to the existing IAS/IPS/IFoS) only on the basis of a Rajya Sabha resolution supported by 2/3 of members present and voting.

8 · What the Rajya Sabha cannot do

By design, the Lok Sabha is supreme in three respects:

Power Lok Sabha Rajya Sabha
Originate a Money Bill ✓ ✗
Vote on Demands for Grants ✓ ✗ (can only discuss)
Pass / move a No-confidence motion against the government ✓ ✗

8.1 Money Bill — the 14-day rule

A Money Bill (Article 110) is introduced only in the Lok Sabha on the President's prior recommendation. After it passes, the Rajya Sabha must return it (with or without recommendations) within 14 days. The Lok Sabha may accept or reject those recommendations. If the Rajya Sabha doesn't act within 14 days, the Bill is deemed passed by both Houses.

8.2 No joint sitting on Money Bills or constitutional amendment bills

The joint sitting mechanism (Article 108) exists to resolve deadlocks between the two Houses on ordinary and financial Bills. It cannot be used for:

  • Money Bills (Lok Sabha is supreme; no deadlock possible).
  • Constitutional Amendment Bills under Article 368 (each House must pass with special majority independently).

There have been only three joint sittings in Indian parliamentary history: Dowry Prohibition Bill (1961), Banking Service Commission (Repeal) Bill (1978), and Prevention of Terrorism Bill (2002).

9 · The 12 nominated members

Article 80(3) reserves 12 nominated seats for individuals with "special knowledge or practical experience in respect of such matters as the following, namely: literature, science, art and social service."

Notable past nominees include scientist Dr. M.S. Swaminathan, jurist L.M. Singhvi, actor Shabana Azmi, sportsperson Sachin Tendulkar, cricketer-statesperson Mary Kom, and writer Ruskin Bond. Nominated members vote in the Vice-Presidential election but not in the Presidential election. They may participate in impeachment of the President.

10 · A "permanent" chamber — why it matters

Three things flow from the Rajya Sabha's permanence:

  1. Continuity. Even when a Lok Sabha is dissolved and a new general election held, the Rajya Sabha continues to function and Parliament can still meet (in the form of the Rajya Sabha + the Council of States proxy).
  2. Counter-majoritarian buffer. A Lok Sabha majority cannot reshape the Rajya Sabha overnight — it takes successive 2-year retirement cycles to flip the composition.
  3. State voice. Even when one party dominates the popular vote nationally, regional parties can preserve a strong presence in the Rajya Sabha through their MLAs.
RS vs LS — the cheat sheet Election: RS = indirect, STV/PR by MLAs · LS = direct, FPTP by voters.
Term: RS = 6 years (1/3 retire every 2 years; permanent body) · LS = 5 years (dissoluble).
Money Bills: RS = can only recommend; 14-day window · LS = exclusive originator.
Confidence: RS = no role · LS = government answers here.
Special powers: RS = Art 249 (state subject), Art 312 (new AIS) · LS = no equivalent exclusives.
Joint sitting (Art 108): Possible on ordinary/financial bills. Not on Money Bills or constitutional amendments.
