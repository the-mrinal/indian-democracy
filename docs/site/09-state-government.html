Anchored in M. Laxmikanth's Indian Polity and Shivam Shankar Singh's How to Win an Indian Election.

The same parliamentary architecture, but mirrored at the state level. A Governor appointed by the President. A Chief Minister who commands the Vidhan Sabha. A Council of Ministers responsible to that Assembly. In six states, a second chamber — the Vidhan Parishad. Most of what citizens experience as "government" — police, roads, schools, hospitals, agriculture — runs through this layer.

Reading time: ~17 min Pairs with: Laxmikanth, Ch. 30–35 · D.D. Basu on State Government

1 · The architecture

Level Constitutional head Real executive Legislature
State Governor Chief Minister + State Council of Ministers Vidhan Sabha (Legislative Assembly) + Vidhan Parishad (Legislative Council, in 6 states)
Union Territory with Legislature Lieutenant Governor / Administrator Chief Minister + Council of Ministers Legislative Assembly
Union Territory without Legislature Administrator / Lt. Governor — (centrally administered) —

The pattern: a ceremonial constitutional head appointed by the Centre, a real executive drawn from the elected legislature, and an Assembly elected directly by the people of the state.

2 · The Governor (Articles 153–162)

2.1 Appointment and tenure

  • Appointed by the President of India under Article 155.
  • Eligibility: citizen of India, age 35+.
  • Convention: not a domicile of the state (to ensure impartiality — honoured in the breach).
  • Holds office during the pleasure of the President (Article 156(1)) — normal term 5 years, but technically removable at any time.

2.2 The doctrine of pleasure — B.P. Singhal (2010)

Removal by President's pleasure is not unfettered. In B.P. Singhal v. Union of India (2010), a Constitution Bench held:

  • Removal is subject to judicial review on grounds of malafides, arbitrariness, or capriciousness.
  • A Governor cannot be removed merely because:
  • they are "out of sync" with the Union government's policies, or
  • there is a change of government at the Centre.
  • The Governor is "not an agent or employee" of the Union.

2.3 Powers

A Governor exercises three layers of power:

  • On the advice of the State Council of Ministers — the bulk of executive functions.
  • Discretionary functions (Article 163(2)) — appointment of CM in a hung assembly, dismissal of a CoM that has lost majority, dissolution, reservation of bills for the President (Art. 200), recommending President's Rule (Art. 356), seeking information from CM (Art. 167).
  • Constitutional powers of assent, summon/prorogue, ordinance under Art. 213.

2.4 Two recent Supreme Court limits

Two judgments in 2023–25 substantially reshaped Governor jurisprudence:

  • State of Punjab v. Principal Secretary to the Governor (November 2023) — A bench led by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud held that Governor Banwarilal Purohit could not "doubt the validity of an Assembly session" or sit on bills indefinitely; Governors are "symbolic heads" who cannot "thwart the normal course of lawmaking" (source).

  • State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor of Tamil Nadu (8 April 2025, 2025 INSC 481) — A bench of Justices J.B. Pardiwala and R. Mahadevan held Tamil Nadu Governor R.N. Ravi's withholding of 10 state bills — some pending since January 2020 — "illegal and erroneous". Key holdings:

  • Under Article 200, the Governor does not have an absolute or pocket veto.
  • Once a Bill is re-passed after reconsideration, the Governor is constitutionally bound to assent.
  • Indicative timelines fixed: 1 month with ministerial advice; 3 months without; 1 month for re-passed bills.
  • Invoking Article 142, the Supreme Court — for the first time in Indian constitutional history — "deemed" 10 state bills to have received the Governor's assent (source).

The combined effect: the Governor's pocket-veto loophole, long a flashpoint between opposition-ruled states and the Centre, is now substantially closed.

3 · The Chief Minister and State Council of Ministers (Articles 163, 164)

The state mirror of the Centre's PM-CoM model.

3.1 Appointment

  • The Governor appoints the CM. Convention: leader of the party or coalition commanding a majority in the Vidhan Sabha.
  • Other Ministers are appointed by the Governor on the CM's advice (Art. 164(1)).
  • Ministers hold office during the pleasure of the Governor — but in practice, the CM's pleasure.

3.2 Size cap

Article 164(1A) (inserted by 91st Amendment, 2003): the total number of Ministers, including the CM, shall not exceed 15 % of the total strength of the Legislative Assembly, subject to a minimum of 12.

3.3 Collective responsibility

The State Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly (Article 164(2)). A vote of no-confidence brings down the entire ministry.

3.4 Anti-defection at state level

The Tenth Schedule applies equally to State legislators. Article 164(1B) (also added by the 91st Amendment) bars a member disqualified under the Tenth Schedule from being appointed Minister.

4 · The State Legislature

4.1 Vidhan Sabha (Legislative Assembly)

  • Article 170: The Assembly has between 60 and 500 members, elected directly by FPTP from territorial constituencies.
  • Term: 5 years from first sitting; dissoluble earlier.
  • Eligibility to contest: Citizen of India, age 25+, name on electoral roll, not disqualified under RPA 1951.
  • Reservation: SC/ST seats per Article 332 (proportional to population). Anglo-Indian nomination (Art. 333) abolished by 104th Amendment, effective 25 January 2020.

Assembly sizes vary hugely: Sikkim (32), Goa (40), Mizoram (40), Arunachal (60) at the small end; Uttar Pradesh (403), Maharashtra (288), West Bengal (294), Bihar (243), Tamil Nadu (234), Madhya Pradesh (230) at the large end.

4.2 Vidhan Parishad (Legislative Council) — only 6 states

A bicameral state legislature exists in only 6 states: Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra, Bihar, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana.

  • Article 169 allows Parliament to create or abolish a Legislative Council if the Assembly so resolves by a special majority.
  • Article 171: The Council strength is not more than 1/3 of the Assembly strength, with a minimum of 40 members.

Composition of the Council (a famously eclectic formula):

Fraction Elected by
1/3 Members of municipalities, district boards and other local authorities
1/12 Graduates of three years' standing
1/12 Teachers of three years' standing in secondary and higher institutions
1/3 Elected by the members of the Legislative Assembly
1/6 Nominated by the Governor for literature, science, art, cooperative movement, social service

The Council is a permanent body: 1/3 retire every 2 years; term 6 years (mirrors the Rajya Sabha).

4.3 The Speaker / Chair

  • Vidhan Sabha has a Speaker and Deputy Speaker, elected from amongst its members.
  • Vidhan Parishad has a Chairperson and Deputy Chairperson, also from amongst its members. (No equivalent of "VP as ex officio Chair" exists at the state level.)

5 · How a state Bill becomes law

The state legislative process tracks the Union closely:

How a State Bill becomes law — 5 stages
01
Introduction
Minister or any MLA introduces in the Vidhan Sabha. Money Bills only in Sabha, on Governor's recommendation.
→
02
Three readings in Vidhan Sabha
First Reading → Committee → Second Reading (clause-by-clause) → Third Reading. Simple majority passes.
→
03
Vidhan Parishad (if bicameral)
UP, MH, Bihar, KA, AP, TG. Council can delay an ordinary bill up to ~4 months; cannot block.
→
04
Governor's options (Art. 200)
Assent · withhold · return (not money bills) · reserve for President. Post-2025 TN judgment: cannot pocket-veto.
→
05
President (Art. 201) or Gazette
If reserved: President assents / withholds / returns for state legislature reconsideration. Else: published as State Act.

5.1 Governor's options (Article 200)

When a Bill is presented to the Governor, four options:

  1. Assent — Bill becomes an Act.
  2. Withhold assent — Bill lapses.
  3. Return for reconsideration (not for Money Bills) — if re-passed, must assent.
  4. Reserve for President's consideration — sent to the President; the President then decides under Article 201 (assent, withhold, or return for state legislative reconsideration with no obligation to assent if re-passed).

5.2 Vidhan Parishad — weaker than Rajya Sabha

The Vidhan Parishad is significantly weaker than the Rajya Sabha:

  • On an ordinary Bill passed by the Assembly and rejected/amended by the Council, the Assembly can re-pass the Bill (with or without accepting Council amendments) after at least one month, and after a second passage, the Bill is deemed passed — the Council is, in effect, a delaying chamber of up to 4 months.
  • The Council has no role at all in Money Bills beyond making recommendations (which the Assembly can ignore).
  • There is no joint sitting mechanism at the state level — the Assembly's voice prevails.

6 · Special Status — Articles 371 to 371-J

Several states and regions enjoy special constitutional provisions under Article 371 and its sub-articles, addressing local sensitivities:

  • Article 371-A — Nagaland: customary law and procedure, land transfers, oil/gas resources protected.
  • Article 371-B — Assam: special committee for the Bodoland Territorial Region.
  • Article 371-C — Manipur: hill areas committee.
  • Article 371-D, 371-E — Andhra Pradesh: equitable opportunities in employment and education across regions.
  • Article 371-F — Sikkim: special provisions after 1975 merger.
  • Article 371-G — Mizoram: religious/social practices, customary law, land ownership.
  • Article 371-H — Arunachal Pradesh: special responsibility of Governor for law and order.
  • Article 371-I — Goa: minimum Assembly strength of 30.
  • Article 371-J — Karnataka: special provisions for Hyderabad-Karnataka region.

The most prominent — Article 370 (Jammu & Kashmir) — was effectively read down on 5 August 2019 by Presidential Order C.O. 272; the abrogation was upheld in the Supreme Court's In Re: Article 370 judgment (December 2023).

7 · The Sixth Schedule — autonomous districts

The Sixth Schedule provides for self-governing Autonomous District Councils in tribal areas of Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram — separate from the Panchayati Raj structure. Each ADC has substantial powers over land, forests, customary law, social customs, money-lending, and inheritance.

8 · Article 356 — President's Rule revisited

President's Rule (already discussed in Chapter 4) is the most consequential intervention the Centre can make in a state's affairs:

  • The President assumes the functions of the State government; the Council of Ministers stands dismissed; the Assembly is suspended or dissolved.
  • Parliament must approve within 2 months; max duration 6 months at a time, extendable to 3 years total under special conditions.
  • S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) brought the proclamation under judicial review; the "majority" of the CoM must be tested on the floor of the Assembly, not in the Raj Bhavan.

The use of Article 356 has fallen dramatically since Bommai — from a routine political tool to an emergency lever rarely employed.
